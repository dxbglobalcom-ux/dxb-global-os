How to Budget for a Website: A Practical Cost Breakdown
=======================================================

[#webdev](/t/webdev)
[#productivity](/t/productivity)
[#business](/t/business)
[#beginners](/t/beginners)

A website quote is useful only when you know what it buys. A five-page brochure site, a searchable catalogue, and an online store may all be called “a website,” yet their design, data, integrations, testing, and maintenance needs are very different.

This guide provides a practical way to build a budget and compare proposals. The figures below are **invented for illustration**; they are not a market survey, a recommended rate, or a quote.

Begin with the outcome, then define the scope
---------------------------------------------

Write down one or two things visitors must accomplish. For a service company, that might be finding an appropriate service and submitting a contact form. For a store, it may be finding an in-stock product and placing an order.

Turn those outcomes into observable acceptance criteria. For example: “A visitor can complete the form on a phone; the responsible person receives the submission; the visitor sees a confirmation.” Add the pages, content, access permissions, and integrations needed to support each criterion.

Count **page templates**, not just URLs. One product template populated with 500 records is a different task from 500 individually designed pages. On the other hand, a site with a handful of pages may require a complex account area or a fragile integration.

Break the build into work packages
----------------------------------

Ask for a written estimate with deliverables and assumptions for each package:

* **Discovery and architecture:** objectives, content inventory, site structure, workflows, and a workable specification.
* **Design:** templates, reusable components, responsive states, forms, and error states.
* **Implementation:** configuration, front-end and back-end work, permissions, and agreed integrations.
* **Content and migration:** writing, images, data cleanup, import, and redirects where applicable.
* **Quality and launch:** acceptance tests, accessibility and device checks, analytics, backups, deployment, and handover.

Coordination and review also consume time. If they are included in another line item, that is fine; the proposal should say so. The same applies to licenses, third-party services, taxes, and work performed by subcontractors.

Build an illustrative estimate
------------------------------

Suppose a team estimates the following hours for a small service website. To keep the arithmetic readable, assume a fictional blended rate of **$80 per hour** for every task. Actual roles can have different rates; use each supplier’s own quote.

| Work package | Hypothetical hours | Illustrative cost |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Discovery and structure | 20 | $1,600 |
| Design and components | 36 | $2,880 |
| Implementation | 72 | $5,760 |
| Content and migration | 24 | $1,920 |
| Testing and launch | 16 | $1,280 |
| **Total labor** | **168** | **$13,440** |

Add a fictional $960 for launch materials and services. The subtotal is $14,400. A contingency of 15% of that subtotal is $2,160, making a planning allowance of **$16,560** before recurring expenses and applicable taxes.

A contingency is not automatically a payment to the contractor. Decide who can approve its use, what constitutes a change, and how unused funds are handled. If requirements are uncertain, ask for a range and list the assumptions that move the estimate up or down. A single precise number based on unknown requirements can be misleading.

Model the first year separately
-------------------------------

A low launch quote can still leave a substantial operating commitment. Convert monthly and annual charges to the same period before comparing them.

A practical first-year model is:

**Build and launch + annualized services + agreed maintenance and improvements.**

Include the items relevant to your project:

1. Domain, hosting, backups, and infrastructure.
2. Commercial themes, plugins, software licenses, and renewals.
3. Monitoring, security updates, support response, and restoration from backup.
4. New content, editorial work, photography, and enhancements.
5. Search optimization, advertising, and measurement, budgeted separately from development.

Check renewal dates and whether the account belongs to the customer. Ask what happens as traffic, catalogue size, or transaction volume grows. Payment and shipping providers may charge according to their own contracts, so request the conditions that apply to your business.

Compare a freelancer, agency, and site builder fairly
-----------------------------------------------------

Give each option the same brief and acceptance criteria. A site builder can reduce initial implementation work when its available features and restrictions fit the job. A self-hosted CMS has a different responsibility model: someone must operate infrastructure, perform updates, and verify backups.

With a freelancer, ask which roles they cover, who can help if they are unavailable, and how code and credentials are handed over. With an agency, identify the delivery lead, the team’s responsibilities, and any subcontractors. In either case, ask whether testing, migration, and project management are included.

A fixed price is easier to compare when the scope is clear. Time-based work can accommodate discovery, provided the customer has a spending limit, transparent reporting, and regular prioritization. A short paid discovery stage can produce the detail needed for a more reliable second estimate.

Questions to settle before signing
----------------------------------

Use the following checklist in a proposal review:

* **Deliverables:** Which templates, workflows, and integrations are included? Who supplies content?
* **Acceptance:** How will you demonstrate that forms, key journeys, mobile layouts, and backups work?
* **Changes:** How many review rounds are included, and who approves additional work before it starts?
* **Payments:** What are the milestones? Are applicable taxes and external charges included?
* **Ownership:** Who controls the domain, hosting account, source files, and analytics? What third-party license terms apply?
* **Support:** What is a defect, what counts as a new feature, and what are the response and restoration arrangements?

Avoid treating predicted sales or search positions as an acceptance test for the build. Verify outcomes the delivery team can demonstrate, such as working forms, documented access, and the agreed templates. Business results depend on the offer, market, and ongoing work as well.

Reduce scope without removing essentials
----------------------------------------

A practical first release might contain the core service pages, contact details, a tested form, and basic analytics. A second language or a custom account area can wait if it is not needed to validate the main journey. Reuse page templates when possible, prepare content early, and run a small test import before committing to a full migration.

Do not save money by silently removing testing or recovery planning. Instead, obtain two or three proposals against the same scope, compare exclusions as carefully as totals, and calculate the first year of ownership. The result is a budget connected to decisions you can actually make.
