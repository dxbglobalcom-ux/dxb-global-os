# Mei Man — for the holding

Studied 2026-11-23. Companion to `PLAYBOOK.md` (section numbers below refer to it). The question behind
this expert: what a first-place ad-creative office looks like when AI workers and our own Media Studio make
the ads — her strategy as the brain, our studio as the hands.

**The short answer.** Her whole practice reduces to one loop that is made of tables and text: listen to
customers → a ranked roadmap of concepts → one belief brief per concept → an asset in a known format → a
read of what the result means → the next roadmap. Every step except the camera is language work, and the
formats she rates highest today need no camera at all. She is now automating that loop herself (October
2026) and still sells it by hand to brands spending $30k+ a month. The holding can run the same loop with
workers, at a cost that reaches the brands she turns away.

**What the board already owns** (checked in `board-rows/B28.md` and `B43.md`, so nothing here is written
twice): the ad-account audit from an export (B28, leg 2026-11-17, claude-ads) · Meta's Ads MCP as a
read-only live eye (B28, leg 2026-11-18) · the weekly loop of research, decode, produce, measure, report for
short video, with the Andromeda risk named (B28, "Format research + the weekly winner") · one variable per
variation (B28, leg 2026-11-18) · pre-publish scores under trial (B28, legs 2026-11-18 and 2026-11-19) · the
brand profile, voice file and style file per client (B28) · the recipe cupboard /kisayol and the testimony
rule R39 (B43). Each idea below says what it **adds** to those.

## The ideas, ranked by value

### 1. The research-to-roadmap engine — the office's brain

- **What.** Her core system (§2, §3): mine what customers say, code it, count it, and turn it into a ranked
  table of about 25 concepts, each with persona, awareness stage, priority and the quotes it stands on;
  refreshed weekly.
- **Where.** The agency seat (B28) — the step **before** its weekly loop. Today that loop starts from what
  already performs (organic outliers, long-running ads in the libraries). By her ranking, that is the
  competitor's surface; the customer's own words are the source of new angles. The two are complements:
  the libraries supply **containers**, the mining supplies **angles**.
- **Form.** A skill for a worker, and a data library per client (the quote bank and the roadmap table).
- **What we build.** The mining run on the research door's own hands (Reddit threads in full and the head
  count, YouTube and TikTok comments, review pages, search phrasing, the brand's and competitors' comment
  sections), writing quote rows with addresses; a coder that tags each quote (persona, stage, emotion,
  belief, objection or desire, the specific situation); a counter; the roadmap table. Skill candidate A.
- **First test.** One of the holding's own lines or the web office's first-case site: one run, one roadmap.
  The lead checks ten concepts by opening their quotes — does each concept really stand on what people
  said? No ad is made in this test.
- **What it earns or improves.** It is what makes every later asset defensible ("this ad exists because 41
  people said this"), and it is saleable alone (idea 8). It also feeds the web office: the same quote bank
  is the raw material for landing-page copy and for the questions an AI-search page must answer.
- **What needs the CEO.** Nothing for the free road. Meta's Muse (her tool; it can search Instagram,
  Facebook and Threads posts from inside a signed-in Meta account) is an identity step if we want to try it
  beside our own door.
- **Risk.** Review and comment text is noisy and skewed to the unhappy; the count must be shown with its
  denominator, never as market size.

### 2. The belief brief as a gate — nothing is produced without it

- **What.** Her three sentences (core belief walking in · reframe · completion thought) plus persona, stage,
  job in the account, one value proposition and the quotes behind it (§5).
- **Where.** Between the agency seat and the Media Studio — the door of every ad job. A studio recipe describes a
  picture; this gate states what the viewer must come to believe, which no image recipe carries.
- **Form.** A gate (a filled form checked before a job is accepted) and a skill that writes it.
- **What we build.** The brief form of §5 as the job's first file; a checker that refuses a brief when a
  field is empty, when the belief or the completion thought traces to no quote row, when there is more than
  one value proposition, or when the concept changes none of the three levers against ads already running
  (idea 3). Skill candidate B.
- **First test.** Take three ads the holding has already made. Write the brief each one *should* have had.
  If the three sentences cannot be written for an ad, that is the finding — and the test of whether the
  gate would have caught it.
- **What it earns or improves.** Fewer rejected films; ads that argue instead of describe; a record of why
  each ad exists, which is what makes a result readable afterwards.
- **What needs the CEO.** Nothing.
- **Risk.** A form can be filled mechanically. The check that matters is the trace to a real quote.

### 3. The portfolio map — a ruler for "is this a new ad or the same one again"

- **What.** Her three levers (funnel stage, mechanic, persona) and the angle above them (§4), with every ad
  tagged by the job it does: creates demand, captures it, or closes (§1). Meta's own December 2025 guidance
  says the delivery system groups look-alike ads as one creative.
- **Where.** The agency seat's weekly loop, step 4. That step says "the winner's variations go into next
  week", and the 18 Nov leg defines a variation as one changed element (cast, hook or call to action). By
  her rule and Meta's, that is **iteration** — right for learning which element carries the ad, wrong as a
  way to grow reach. The row already names the Andromeda risk; this is the ruler that enforces it.
- **Form.** A data library (one row per ad: concept, persona, stage, mechanic, angle, job) and a gate.
- **What we build.** The tag set written into the asset's name (§9.3); a check at brief time — "did we
  change who we talk to, how the message is delivered, or where they are in the funnel?"; a weekly coverage
  table per client: personas × stages, with empty cells named, and the share of spend on ads that create
  demand against ads that capture it.
- **First test.** Tag the public ads of three brands from the Meta ad library by hand with the tag set. Does
  the table show at a glance what she claims to see in five minutes — an account that is all one stage, one
  persona, one format? That same table is the free sample of idea 5.
- **What it earns or improves.** It turns "diversify" from advice into a countable state, and it is the
  picture a prospect understands in ten seconds.
- **What needs the CEO.** Nothing.
- **Risk.** Tags are judgements; two workers must tag the same ad the same way before the table is trusted.

### 4. The format cupboard — her mechanics as recipes the studio can make without a camera

- **What.** About twenty-five containers with their rules (§8): text-only video with matched audio, notes
  app, email, post-it, testimonial static, us-versus-them, split screen, moving grid, comment reply, POV,
  negative hook, villain-the-category, listicle, and the Q4 list (search box, countdown, strike-through,
  tweet, apology note, value stack, "do not buy this", Reddit post, poem…).
- **Where.** The studio's recipe cupboard (/kisayol, B43) — a shelf of **ad formats**, beside its image and
  camera recipes.
- **Why this fits us unusually well.** The cupboard's first twelve samples were rejected because pictures
  made from text alone look made by a machine. Most of her formats are not pictures: a note, an email, a
  search box, a comparison table, words over sound. They can be **drawn in code** (HTML to image, or the
  motion-graphics door for the moving ones), exact to the pixel, with no model in the loop and nothing to
  look artificial. Her rule for them is that they look like the private surfaces people already read.
- **Form.** Recipes (one file each: what it is, when it fits which stage, her rules, the slots it takes
  from the brief, how it is rendered, what it must never do) and code templates.
- **What we build.** First five, all faceless: notes (light and dark), email (founder as sender),
  us-versus-them, text-only vertical video, split screen with one real product clip. Each takes its words
  from a brief, never from a blank prompt. The post-it is the one exception that needs a hand: she insists
  on real handwriting photographed on a phone — for our own brands that is a real photo, not a render.
- **First test.** One brief (idea 2) rendered into the five formats for one holding product; his eye on the
  five. The measure is the one already his: does it look real.
- **What it earns or improves.** A low-cost first product tier that does not wait for film quality; volume
  with a reason behind each asset; the answer to "what do we make this week" for any client.
- **What needs the CEO.** His eye on the first set. Music: the cupboard's standing rule (B43 R34) leaves
  music to the first client; her text-only format lives or dies on the audio matching the emotion, so that
  decision comes due with this format.
- **Risk.** Formats wear out — she says so ("trends die"). The cupboard records when a format was last seen
  working, and the angle always comes from the roadmap.

### 5. The funnel-leak read — the bridge between the ad office and the web office

- **What.** Her ordered read of an account (§9.2): page loads per click → add to cart → checkout started →
  purchase, plus the 30-second structure check (§9.3) and the "what job does each ad do" read (§1).
- **Where.** The audit door-opener the agency seat already has (B28, claude-ads). That tool checks account
  hygiene; her read adds the two things it does not: **where the money leaks after the click**, and
  **whether the creative portfolio can scale at all**. And it gives the web office its strongest lead:
  three of her five steps are website problems — the page that does not load, the page that does not sell,
  the checkout that loses trust. Her own complaint is that clients' landing pages are broken and nobody
  owns them. We own a web office.
- **Form.** A skill (candidate C) that reads an export and writes a one-page diagnosis, each finding routed
  to the office that fixes it: creative → studio, page → web studio, tracking → the setup sprint.
- **What we build.** The read as rules over an Ads Manager export and a store report; the routing; the
  one-page report.
- **First test.** The holding has no ad export of its own (B28 says so). Until one exists: run the page
  half on the first-case site (does the page load fast on a phone, does its first screen match what an ad
  would promise, are proof and price reframing present) — that half needs no ad account.
- **What it earns or improves.** One audit sells two offices. It is also the honest answer to "the ads don't
  work": by her ranking the page is changed only on evidence, and this read is the evidence.
- **What needs the CEO.** Nothing for an export. A live account connection is an identity step (already
  written in B28).
- **Risk.** Her thresholds are from single accounts; the report states a client's own numbers against their
  own order value and CAC, not against "industry averages" (her own rule).

### 6. The winner read — "why did it win" before anything is repeated

- **What.** Her questions after a result (§4, §1): was it the message, the format, or luck; was this ad the
  creator of demand or only the closer; what was running around it; what does that say about the next brief.
  The site names it as a product: "agents that turn winners into the next round of briefs."
- **Where.** The weekly loop's Monday step (B28) — between "pick the winner" and "next week".
- **Form.** A skill and a short written verdict per winner and per notable loser.
- **What we build.** Skill candidate D: it reads the ad's tags, its brief, the week's numbers and what else
  ran; states one hypothesis for the win and the cheapest test that would separate message from format
  (the same message in a second mechanic, or the same mechanic with a second message); writes the next
  brief from the answer.
- **First test.** On the holding's own published videos once the weekly loop has two weeks of numbers.
- **What it earns or improves.** It stops the commonest waste she describes — ten copies of the closer.
- **What needs the CEO.** Nothing.
- **Risk.** With little spend, a "why" is a guess; the verdict must say how sure it is and name the test.

### 7. Her note for the pre-publish score trials

- **What.** Her 2026 statement that hook rate, hold rate and CTR "do not predict performance at scale" and
  that some of her best ads had worse numbers than the losers (§9.1).
- **Where.** The two score trials already on the board (B28: the Virality-Predictor-style score, and TRIBE
  v2). The TRIBE trial's bar is already the right one — it must rank known sellers above known flops. Her
  statement adds one line to both trials: include ads that **sold** with weak attention numbers, if any can
  be found, because that is the case where an attention score would wrongly stop a good ad.
- **Form.** A line in the trial design. Nothing to build.
- **What needs the CEO.** Nothing.
- **Risk.** Her claim is unpublished experience; it is a reason to test, not a finding.

### 8. What we sell — three offers taken from how she earns

- **What.** Her funnel (§14) has three parts we can run: a diagnostic that opens the door, a strategy
  document that is valuable alone, and done-for-you production on a monthly system.
- **Where.** Revenue lines (B83) sold from the agency seat (B28).
- **Form.** Offers:
  1. **The portfolio map** (idea 3) of a prospect's public ads — the free sample. It needs no access, shows
     the empty cells, and is specific to them. It is the ad-office twin of the web office's firm-specific
     demo.
  2. **The creative roadmap** as a fixed-price deliverable: the quote bank, 25 ranked concepts, five full
     briefs. Useful to a brand with its own production, and the natural first paid step.
  3. **The monthly system**: roadmap refresh, a set number of assets from the format cupboard and the
     studio, the Monday read (idea 6), and the leak read each month (idea 5).
- **Who.** She takes brands above $30k a month in ad spend and sends the rest a polite refusal on the form
  itself. Everything below that line is a market she has declared she will not serve — and it is where a
  worker-run office has its cost advantage. Her faceless formats are also what a small brand can afford.
- **What we build.** The application form in her shape (ten questions, a qualifying band, an answer on the
  spot) for our own bands; the three offers written once.
- **First test.** The map made for five real brands in one sector, unsent; the lead judges whether each
  would make its owner want the next step.
- **What it earns or improves.** A sales path where each step proves the next.
- **What needs the CEO.** The prices (she publishes none — nothing to copy); whether a results guarantee is
  offered (hers in 2023: "no better results in 90 days, we work for free"); ad spend is always the client's
  money in the client's account, and any spend of ours is money out.
- **Risk.** Named once: results in ads depend on the client's product, page and budget; a guarantee ties our
  income to things we do not control unless the leak read (idea 5) is a condition of it.

### 9. The content she uses to win clients — forms we can run on the holding's channels

- **What.** Seven repeating reel forms (§14). The two that travel best and need no client results: the
  **teardown** of a well-known brand's ad (what works, what to fix, a score, "a 7, an easy 9 with these
  changes") and the **tier ranking** ("things brands do when results fall, ranked").
- **Where.** The agency seat's own channel, made by the social-media team and the studio.
- **Form.** A content format with a fixed skeleton.
- **What we build.** The teardown skeleton: the ad's claim → hook → what the viewer believes at each beat →
  the moment conviction is lost → three fixes → score. The worker fills it from the playbook's rules; the
  studio renders it as a split screen (the ad beside the notes) — one of her own formats.
- **First test.** Three teardowns written, unpublished; the lead checks each fix against the playbook.
- **What it earns or improves.** Proof of thinking before there is proof of results — the position the
  holding is in today.
- **What needs the CEO.** Which account publishes them, and when (an identity step). Showing another
  brand's ad inside ours is commentary; the tone stays respectful — hers is not, and that is her brand, not
  the method.
- **Risk.** A teardown without results behind it invites "who are you"; the first ones should be of ads
  whose outcome is public.

### 10. The seasonal calendar

- **What.** Her Q4 rules (§10): test before the event, scale the proven during it, keep the evergreen ads
  running, make the offer readable at a glance, mostly statics, speak to people already warmed.
- **Where.** The agency seat's client calendar; it applies to every sale event in every market, not only
  Black Friday.
- **Form.** A checklist with dates counted back from the event: eight weeks out the roadmap, six to three
  weeks out the tests, the last week only winners and offer statics from the cupboard's Q4 shelf.
- **What needs the CEO.** Nothing. Her bid-cap rule ("ten times CPA") is left out — untested and risky (§10).

### 11. The compliance gate for ads

- **What.** The lines her advice crosses (§11) written as checks: no invented testimonial, comment or
  sender; no before-and-after or promised result in health, appearance and money categories; no false claim
  about a category or a rival; nothing outside the holding's content boundary.
- **Where.** The studio's pre-delivery check, beside R39.
- **Form.** A gate. It adds to R39 the cases R39 does not name: comment bubbles, email senders, quoted
  review text, and comparison claims.
- **What we build.** A checklist the checker reads against the finished ad and its brief: every quoted
  customer line has a source row; every "comment" is real or labelled as ours; every claim about results
  has its basis; the category attack has its argument written in the brief.
- **What it earns or improves.** Accounts that are not shut down — her own story is that such mistakes cost
  clients "millions".
- **What needs the CEO.** Nothing.

## The larger picture — a first-place ad-creative office on this

Picture the office as a loop with five rooms, each with one worker's job and one object it hands on.

**The listening room.** Every week, for every client, a worker reads what the market is saying — reviews,
threads, comments, search phrasing — and adds to a quote bank. Nothing here is written by us; it is
collected and counted. The object: a bank of real sentences with addresses.

**The strategy room.** A worker turns the bank into the roadmap: twenty-five concepts, each a persona at a
stage of awareness with a belief to change, ranked by how often and how strongly people said it. Beside it
hangs the portfolio map — the grid of persona by stage with every running ad placed on it. The empty cells
are next week's work. This room is what a client is really buying, and it is the room she guards for
herself.

**The brief desk.** Each chosen concept becomes one page: what they believe now, what they will see, what
they must believe at the end, one value, one emotion, the format, the quotes it stands on. The desk refuses
a brief that is the same ad again. This is the gate; the studio does not start without the page.

**The studio.** The hands. For the faceless formats, code draws the asset exactly — a note, an email, a
table, words on a beat. For film, the studio's own lanes. Because the brief names the belief and the
emotion, the studio is judged on one question: does the viewer arrive at the completion thought. The
compliance gate reads every asset before it leaves.

**The reading room.** Each Monday a worker reads the numbers in her order — did people arrive, did the page
sell, did they pay — and writes for each winner and loser why, with what confidence, and what test would
settle it. Page problems go to the web office as jobs. The verdicts go back to the strategy room, and the
loop turns.

Three things make this first-place rather than a copy of her agency.

*It closes the loop she cannot.* Her recurring complaint is the client's broken landing page that nobody
owns. Here the ad office and the web office are one house: the leak the reading room finds on Monday is a
web job on Tuesday, and the roadmap's quote bank writes the page as well as the ad. No creative agency and
no web agency offers both halves from one research base.

*It serves the market she refuses.* Her loop by hand needs a brand spending $30k a month to pay for it. Run
by workers, with formats drawn in code, the same loop fits a brand spending a tenth of that. She has
published where her floor is; everything under it is open.

*It keeps its reasons.* Every asset carries its concept, its brief and its quotes in its name and its
record. After a year the office holds something no human agency keeps: for every ad, why it existed, what
it was meant to change, and what happened — per sector, per persona, per format. That record is the
holding's compounding asset, and it is what makes the next client's first roadmap better than the last
client's fifth.

What it does not replace: someone must still be able to say **why** a result happened and what to try next
— she is right that this is the hard part. The office's answer is not to guess faster, but to write the
hypothesis down before the spend and read it back after.

## Skill candidates

Each is written so the skill can be authored from it. Names are working names.

### A. `ad-voice-mining` — the quote bank and the roadmap

- **Triggers when** a new ad client or brand is opened; weekly for an active one; when a roadmap has no
  untested high-priority concept left.
- **Inputs.** The brand's site and product pages; its review sources; three to five competitors; the
  category's name in customers' words; the markets and languages served; the existing quote bank and
  roadmap, if any.
- **Steps.**
  1. List the sources for this brand: own reviews, each competitor's reviews (lowest stars first), forum
     threads on the problem and on the category, comments under the brand's and competitors' posts and ads,
     comments under creator videos on the category, search suggestions and question phrasing.
  2. Fetch each through the research door; write every usable sentence as a quote row — verbatim, with
     address, author if public, date, source type. Never paraphrase.
  3. Code each row: persona (who, buying for what reason), awareness stage (one of five), emotion, the
     belief it shows, objection or desire, the specific situation in it, language.
  4. Cluster rows by belief and persona. For each cluster count rows and distinct authors; rate emotional
     strength on a fixed three-step scale with the two strongest quotes as the evidence.
  5. Write one concept per strong cluster: name, one-line instruction, persona portrait in one line, stage,
     priority (frequency × emotion), the job it would do in the account, the quote ids.
  6. Lay the concepts on the persona × stage grid; mark cells with no concept; add concepts until every
     stage is served, or state that the evidence does not support one.
  7. Mark which concepts are new against last week and which quotes are new.
- **Output.** `quotes.jsonl` (the bank) · `roadmap.md` (the table in her column order, about 25 rows, each
  with its counts) · `coverage.md` (the grid and its empty cells) · a short list of what was looked for and
  not found.
- **Must never.** Invent or tidy a quote; present a count as market size; take an angle from a competitor's
  ad rather than from a customer's words.

### B. `ad-belief-brief` — one concept into one page, with the gate

- **Triggers when** a roadmap concept is chosen for production; when a result (skill D) orders a new variant.
- **Inputs.** The concept row and its quote rows; the client's brand profile and voice file; the portfolio
  map of what is running; the product's real facts, price and offer; the category's ad restrictions.
- **Steps.**
  1. Write the three sentences: core belief walking in (in the customer's words, from a quote), the reframe
     (what they will see, and whether it is a promise or a proof), the completion thought.
  2. Fix persona, stage, and the job in the account (creates, captures, closes).
  3. Choose one value proposition and one emotion; name the objection the ad answers.
  4. Choose the mechanic from the format cupboard that suits the stage; state why.
  5. Write the hook (its type from the six) and the four parts of the value section — emotional discovery,
     problem recognition, desire, de-risking proof — as beats, not as a script yet.
  6. Run the same-ad check against the portfolio map: persona, mechanic or stage must differ from every
     running ad of this concept; otherwise say plainly that this is an iteration and what it is meant to
     learn.
  7. Run the compliance checks (idea 11) on claims, quotes and comparisons.
  8. Write what a win and a loss would each mean.
- **Output.** `brief.md` in the form of PLAYBOOK §5, the asset's name in the tag convention, and a pass or a
  refusal with the reason.
- **Must never.** Pass a brief whose belief or completion thought has no quote behind it; carry two value
  propositions; attribute words to a customer who did not say them.

### C. `ad-funnel-leak-read` — where the money is lost

- **Triggers when** a prospect or client hands over an account export; monthly for an active client; when
  CAC rises two weeks running.
- **Inputs.** An Ads Manager export at ad level with link clicks, landing-page views, add-to-cart,
  checkout started, purchases, spend, impressions, 3-second plays; the store's own revenue and orders for
  the same dates; average order value; the date range, currency and attribution setting.
- **Steps.**
  1. State the attribution setting and compare platform-reported revenue with store revenue; if they differ
     widely, say so first.
  2. Read in order and stop at the first broken step: landing-page views ÷ link clicks → add-to-cart rate →
     checkout-started rate → add-to-cart to purchase → click to purchase. Report the client's own figures
     and their AOV-to-CAC relation; use her bands only as questions.
  3. Structure check: campaigns doing the same job; ad sets with no one-sentence reason; mixed countries;
     very small budgets; arbitrary caps; naming that carries no meaning.
  4. Portfolio check: tag each ad with spend by persona, stage, mechanic and job; show the share of spend on
     ads that create demand, and the empty cells.
  5. Route each finding: creative → a brief request; page load, page persuasion or checkout trust → a web
     office job with the evidence; tracking → the setup sprint.
  6. Rank the findings by money, largest first.
- **Output.** A one-page diagnosis: the first leak, its size in the client's money, who fixes it; then the
  rest in order; then what could not be read from the export.
- **Must never.** Recommend a page change without evidence the leak is after the click; quote an industry
  average as a target; change anything in an account.

### D. `ad-winner-read` — why it won, and the next brief

- **Triggers when** the weekly numbers are in; when an ad passes a spend threshold set per client; before any
  winner is repeated.
- **Inputs.** The ad's brief and tags; its numbers by day; what else ran in the account and in organic in
  the same period; the portfolio map.
- **Steps.**
  1. State the ad's job from its tags and judge it against that job only.
  2. List what ran around it; say whether it most likely created demand or closed demand others created,
     and what would show the difference (new-customer share, performance when the surrounding ads paused).
  3. Give one hypothesis for the win — message, mechanic, persona fit, or chance — with its confidence and
     the spend it rests on.
  4. Name the cheapest test that separates message from mechanic: the same brief in a second mechanic, or
     the same mechanic with a second concept.
  5. Order the next briefs: one that moves the winner along a lever (stage, mechanic or persona), one for
     the largest empty cell on the map.
  6. For a notable loser: which of the four value-section parts was missing, read against the brief.
- **Output.** A verdict of a few lines per ad, the test, and brief requests for skill B; a line added to the
  client's record of what has been learned.
- **Must never.** Order "more like it" without naming which lever changes; treat a hook or hold number as
  proof of sales.

### E. `ad-format-render` — a brief into a faceless asset

- **Triggers when** a passed brief names a code-drawn format (notes, email, us-versus-them, text-only video,
  search box, strike-through, value stack and the others on that shelf).
- **Inputs.** The brief; the brand profile (fonts, colours, logo); real product images where the format
  shows the product; the placement sizes; for video, the audio chosen for the brief's emotion under the
  studio's music rule.
- **Steps.**
  1. Take the words from the brief; write the on-screen copy in the customer's register, within the
     format's limits (a notes headline of 10-12 words with two or three short lines; a testimonial of at
     most three lines; one comparison table of few rows).
  2. Render from the format's template in code at each placement size; for the notes format both light and
     dark; for video, time the lines to the audio.
  3. Check the render against the format's own rules (palette limit, equal clip lengths in a grid, the
     comment answered in the first three seconds, the offer readable at a glance).
  4. Run the realism check — does it look like the surface it borrows — and the compliance gate.
- **Output.** The asset files named by the tag convention, a contact sheet, and the checks' results.
- **Must never.** Fill a format from a blank prompt without a brief; draw a handwritten note by machine and
  pass it as handwriting; show an invented person's comment, review or email.

### F. `ad-teardown` — the holding's own content form

- **Triggers when** the agency seat's content calendar calls for one; when a widely seen ad appears in a
  sector we want clients from.
