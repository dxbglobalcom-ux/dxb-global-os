## 1. The core idea — an ad account is an ecosystem, and creative is the targeting

**[HER]**

- "Creative is your targeting" (`mei-DNjFVKnJQ4T @02:48`; first stated 2023, `meyt--E0iBvhD4AI @03:45`:
  "your creatives filter out your customers from people who will never buy from you").
- "Nothing in your ad account exists in isolation. Every creative is either generating demand, capturing
  that demand, or riding the coattails of something else that's doing the heavy lifting. If you don't know
  which one you're looking at, you will scale the wrong thing every single time." (`mei-DV9AwX2ETm5 @01:49`)
- "Your static didn't win. Your ecosystem won." A winning static is usually **the closer**: videos, organic
  posts and other ads created the demand, the static captured it. Ten copies of the closer are "a closer
  with nothing left to close." (`mei-DV9AwX2ETm5 @00:57-02:09`)
- "Videos create demand. Statics capture that demand." "90% of your creative should create demand, not
  capture it." Most brands make all their ads for the 5% who already know them or are actively looking —
  "fishing in a bathtub and then getting mad you can't catch a shark." (`mei-DTTQ6skEYVD @00:05-01:19`)
- Exception she names: a brand with heavy recognition from elsewhere (her example: Hims) can run statics
  forever, because Meta is only doing its retargeting (`mei-DTTQ6skEYVD @03:10`).
- Her case: spend doubled year on year ($50k at 9.87 ROAS → $117k at 9.9 ROAS) with **fewer** ads (538 →
  465). The top six ads by spend were all upper-funnel, for "people who don't even realize a problem
  exists" (`mei-DSqEedWkZoS @00:13-01:56`).

**[TEST] True and current — this is Meta's own position.** Meta, 16 Dec 2025, "Demystifying Creative
Diversification" (business.meta.com/news/demystifying-creative-diversification/): creative iteration (same
visual, different text) is not diversification; the delivery system "groups together ads that share key
visual and thematic attributes" and treats look-alike ads as variations of one creative; advertisers should
"develop ads that are truly different in look, feel, storyline, and message" for "different personas or use
cases". Her Pokémon rule (§4) is the same statement, eighteen months of practice ahead of the page.

**[TEST] Where it is weak.** "90%" and "5%" are rhetoric, not measurements; the 5% echoes the common
"95-5" B2B rule (most of a market is not buying now), which she does not cite. "Videos create, statics
capture" is a tendency in her accounts, not a law — her own best cold-traffic examples include statics
(the pontoon fender line, §6). The sound version: **tag every ad by the job it does (creates demand /
captures / closes) and judge it against that job**, whatever its format.

## 2. Research — where every ad starts

**[HER]**

- "Real research means mining thousands of reviews, Reddit threads, social comments, and not just yours —
  your competitors'. Their one-star reviews, that's your ad copy. Their customer frustrations, that's your
  hook." (`mei-DWEt_ieEQKG @00:47-01:04`)
- Copy must use "words and language that your customers actually use in real life"; pull "exact phrases and
  keywords" from reviews, competitors' reviews and comment sections (`mei-DMvl5lPJJh8 @00:53-02:01`).
- The best raw material is the customer's **inner monologue** — "the thing they desperately want to say out
  loud but can't articulate themselves" (`mei-DeKLEO3sNsn @00:40-00:56`); "maximum resonance, where the
  viewer feels like you're reading their minds" (`mei-DSGHOztkasS @03:17`).
- Micro-specific beats general: "POV: you spent $1,400 on products that promise to fix your dark spots but
  somehow made them worse, then tried this $34 one out of desperation and watched them fade in nine days"
  instead of "POV: this serum is amazing" (`mei-DSGHOztkasS @03:24-03:44`; the same rule for POV hooks,
  `mei-DKu54udpQpZ @01:24-02:20`).
- New angles "must come from real signal: customer language, market behavior, or performance data" — not
  "give me 20 angles in ChatGPT" (`mei-DdozG77RwW7 @03:00-03:08`).
- Competitor spying is not research: "that is how you stay stuck at a two ROAS forever"
  (`mei-DWEt_ieEQKG @00:39`). Copying a competitor's winner is "D tier": you do not see their offer,
  audience, affiliates or funnel, and "the strategy behind that ad was developed months before you ever saw
  it" (`mei-DdozG77RwW7 @00:02-00:38`).
- Her current tooling (Oct 2026): a weekly-running "super creative strategist" built on Meta's Muse, which
  "mines hundreds of posts" on Instagram, Facebook and Threads, watches the videos as well as reading the
  comments, and outputs "a complete roadmap that spans 25 unique concepts, complete with personas, awareness
  stages, priority, ranked by emotion and frequency", with "full briefs with the scripts already written,
  traced back to real customer language" (`mei-Dd_4CMkM-1v @00:02-00:59`). The site names four listening
  sources: ads and video, comments and posts, reviews, search and trends (meimedia.com, "How it works").

**[ADDED] What Muse is.** Meta's model Muse Spark, released 8 Apr 2026 on meta.ai. Its chat harness exposes
`meta_1p.content_search` — "semantic search across Instagram, Threads, and Facebook posts", only posts the
signed-in user may view, created since 2025-01-01 — plus media download into a sandbox and sub-agents
(Simon Willison, simonwillison.net/2026/Apr/8/muse-spark/). That is the capability she is using; it needs a
Meta login. Transcript note: the reel's opening words are misheard ("Quadrant chat APT") — she says Claude
and ChatGPT.

**[ADDED] The half she does not give — the mining procedure.** She names sources and outputs, never the
steps. A complete version, built from her statements:

1. Collect verbatim text per source: the brand's reviews (all stars), each competitor's reviews (1-3 stars
   first), Reddit threads on the problem and the category, comments under the brand's and competitors' posts
   and ads, comments under creator videos about the category, search suggestions and question phrasing.
2. Keep each quote whole, with its address. Never paraphrase at this step.
3. Code each quote: persona (who is speaking and why they buy), awareness stage (§3), the emotion, the
   belief it reveals (§5), objection or desire, and the micro-specific situation in it.
4. Count: how often each theme occurs (frequency) and how strongly it is felt (emotion). Her roadmap ranks
   by exactly these two.
5. Turn each strong cluster into one concept row (§3). Every concept cites its quotes.
6. Repeat weekly — "people never stop posting".

**[TEST]** The method is sound and is ordinary voice-of-customer research done at volume. Two limits:
(a) one-star reviews of a competitor show what that competitor's *unhappy* buyers say — they find angles,
they do not size demand; (b) her line "if you don't have any reviews, just look at your competitors and
paraphrase some of the best ones" as a quoted testimonial is a fake testimonial — see §11.

## 3. The creative roadmap — the document that drives production

**[HER]** The roadmap "covers various concepts, angles, customer personas, stages of awareness, because
we're never guessing. We're engineering." (`mei-DWEt_ieEQKG @01:09`). Every creative "needs to match a stage
of awareness" — she calls this **funnel-proofing** (`mei-DSqEedWkZoS @01:16-01:47`).

The site shows the table's shape (meimedia.com, "Research what already converts"). Columns: number ·
concept · the one-line instruction · awareness stage · priority · persona with a one-line portrait. Her
eight sample rows:

| # | Concept | Instruction | Awareness | Priority | Persona |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 01 | "I was skeptical too" | Name the objection before they do | Solution aware | High | Burned-before buyer — tried three products that let her down |
| 02 | The gift reaction | Let the emotion sell the gift | Problem aware | Medium-high | Last-minute gifter — wants thoughtful without the research |
| 03 | "I'd tried everything" | Open on the pain, then prove it's different | Unaware | High | Frustrated researcher — trusts nothing that sounds like an ad |
| 04 | Ingredient breakdown | Proof beats claims at the last step | Product aware | Medium | Label reader |
| 05 | Founder POV | Trust closes the sale | Most aware | High | Values-driven buyer |
| 06 | The math | Reframe the price with category math | Product aware | Medium-high | Price-sensitive shopper — believes the category overcharges |
| 07 | Myth-bust | Steelman the loudest skeptic in the comments | Solution aware | Medium | Cynic in the comments |
| 08 | A day in the life | Sell the routine around the product | Problem aware | High | Busy routine-dropper |

Then a status board per concept (ROAS · spend · Testing / scaling) and a month-over-month line (ROAS,
revenue, CAC). The cycle on the site: research → roadmap → launch, iterate, scale; "when the read on a
winner is wrong, the next ad is a coin flip."

**[ADDED]** The five stages are Eugene Schwartz's (Breakthrough Advertising, 1966): unaware → problem aware
→ solution aware → product aware → most aware. She uses them without naming him. What each stage needs:

| Stage | The viewer | The ad's job | Her formats that fit |
|---|---|---|---|
| Unaware | Does not feel the problem | Emotional discovery; a story or identity they recognise | Text-only video, POV, day-in-the-life, villain-the-category |
| Problem aware | Feels it, knows no fix | Name the problem better than they can | "I'd tried everything", negative hook, POV |
| Solution aware | Knows kinds of fix | Why this kind, why the others fail | Us-vs-them, myth-bust, comment reply, split screen |
| Product aware | Knows the product, unsure | Proof, price reframing, objections | Testimonial static, ingredient breakdown, the math, email ad |
| Most aware | Ready | Offer, reminder | Offer static, post-it, notes ad |

**[TEST]** Sound. The roadmap is the single most transferable object in her practice: it is a table, its
inputs are countable, and every later decision (what to produce, what a result means) reads from it.

## 4. Iterating a winner — the three levers (the "Charizard" rule)

**[HER]** (`mei-DdEvQLos64F`, whole) Rewording a winner makes "a clone with the exact same strengths and the
exact same weaknesses"; both die of the same audience fatigue. Three real options:

1. **Change the funnel stage.** A product-aware winner rebuilt for problem-aware reaches cold traffic
   instead of the same retargeting pool.
2. **Change the mechanic** — how the message is delivered. "Customer POV becomes a founder confession;
   problem-solution becomes a listicle. Same core message, completely different execution."
3. **Change the persona** — who is spoken to and what moves them. "The mom buying for 20 minutes of peace is
   not the same guy buying to save 40 bucks."

The test before any variant ships: **"Did we change who we're talking to, how we're delivering the message,
or where they are in the funnel? If the answer is no, it's not a new variant."**

And before iterating at all, know **why** it won: "was it the message, the format, or did the creator just
get lucky on one take?" (`mei-Dd_4CMkM-1v @01:22`). "Make more like your winning ads is not a real strategy"
(`lists/mei-ig-captions.md`, 2026-03-16).

A fourth, larger lever sits above these: **a new angle or motivation** — her S tier. "A new execution gives
you another ad. A new motivation gives you a new lane to scale." "10 executions of the same angle is still 10
bets on the same idea." (`mei-DdozG77RwW7 @02:46-03:13`)

**[TEST]** True, and confirmed by Meta's Dec 2025 page (§1). Note the direct consequence for any "change
only the hook / only the CTA" variation tool: by her rule and Meta's, those are iterations — useful for
learning which element carries the ad, useless for reach.

## 5. The brief — belief first

**[HER]** (`mei-DfAH9zEMy4K`, whole) Most teams stop at problem recognition ("what problem does our product
solve") and identity recognition ("who has it"). Winning ads add **belief recognition**. Three questions,
answered before anything is "written, scripted or produced":

1. **Core belief walking in** — what does this person currently believe?
2. **The reframe** — what do they need to see to change it? (and in what form — a promise, or proof?)
3. **Completion thought** — what must they believe when the ad ends?

Her worked example, "Lake House Mom" (a water toy; $24k spend at 10.6 ROAS, her figure):

| Field | Content |
|---|---|
| Core belief | "My kids can have fun, but only if I'm babysitting them the whole time. It's fun for them, not for me." |
| Reframe | "Not a promise that she's allowed to relax, but proof delivered as a surprise." |
| Completion thought | "My kids played on this thing for hours straight and I got to relax." |
| The line in the ad | "My teenagers who are too cool for everything spent five hours on this thing acting like little kids." |

"We didn't agitate the problem, list benefits or features. She changed what she believed about her own day."

**The value section of a video — four parts, "the minimum bar"** (`mei-DVJqJuBEYnh @01:36-02:21`):

1. **Emotional discovery** — "expose them, make it feel personal… 'oh f—, this is me'. If it doesn't do
   that, nothing else in the ad matters."
2. **Problem recognition** — force them to admit they cannot ignore it any more.
3. **Desire activation** — "they don't just need to understand the solution, they need to want it."
4. **Proof that de-risks the decision** — "anything that triggers 'I don't trust this' or 'I'm not sure it's
   worth it' kills the sale."

Her three questions for any angle (`@03:00`): what emotion does it tap, does it make someone feel seen, does
it reframe a belief. "If the answer is no, I don't care what your hook or hold rate is, it's not a scalable
asset."

**Conviction transfer** — from her teardown of an Anthropic ad for Claude (`mei-DdZVDSxsiSo`, whole). What
she praised: the hook qualifies the audience at once, one angle only ("most brands cram 10 use cases into
one ad"), a look made by a creator rather than a brand. What she would fix: show the real mess (the
47-item notes list, the desk with 22 sticky notes) dropped into the product, so the transformation is seen;
at the product's strongest moment the presenter walks away calmly — "this is where you lose your s— on
camera"; the ending ("so I can focus on what matters…") "was written by a marketer". Her rule: "If you're
selling me something that sounds almost too good to be true, the hardest part is making me believe it." She
scored it 7, "an easy 9" with the fixes.

**Emotion over feature, with her wording examples** (`mei-DTTQ6skEYVD @02:40-03:10`): for a weight-loss
brand, instead of features — "You're not cheating, it's just that you miss feeling good in your own body";
"the version of you that doesn't overthink every outfit or every photo."

**The work she says hides behind one 60-second ad** (`mei-DWEt_ieEQKG @00:04-00:23`): 26 drafts killed,
eight hook directions scrapped so three survive, one line rewritten 13 times, a script rebuilt five ways.
"I spent 11 hours on a creative brief" (caption, 2026-03-19).

**[ADDED] The brief as a form** — her fields put in one place, so a worker can fill it:

```
concept (roadmap row)        persona (portrait in one line)        awareness stage
job in the account           creates demand | captures | closes
core belief walking in       (a quote row from research)
reframe                      (what they see; promise or proof)
completion thought           (one sentence, in the customer's words)
the one value proposition    (one only)
emotion to land              (and the audio that carries it)
mechanic / format            (from the format library, section 6)
hook                         (type, section 7; the first 3 seconds)
value section                emotional discovery · problem recognition · desire · proof
objection answered           (price? trust? "is it for me?")
evidence                     (ids of the customer quotes it is built on)
what result would prove      (what a win or a loss will teach)
```

**[TEST]** The belief brief is the strongest piece of craft in the material: it is short, it is checkable
(either the three sentences exist and trace to a real quote, or they do not), and it prevents the commonest
failure — an ad that lists benefits to someone who does not yet believe the premise. It descends from
classic direct response (belief-shift copy); she does not claim to have invented it. Her ROAS figures beside
it are single ads from her accounts and prove nothing general.

## 6. Angles that she says win

### 6.1 Make the category the villain (`mei-DecLfuoMoLq`, whole)

**[HER]** Ads that list benefits ("three benefits of…", "X is better than Y because…") persuade nobody. The
ones that scale attack how the whole category works. Her three winners:

- "First fender made for pontoons." — top ad by spend and ROAS "by a mile".
- "Your vulva isn't supposed to be cleaned with a fruity body wash." — cheapest CAC in the account.
- "I priced every organic mattress so you don't have to. Here's what no one tells you about the expensive
  ones." — "scaled harder than anything else we've run."

The question to ask: **"What does everyone in your category accept as normal that actually sucks?"** Why it
works on cold traffic: a stranger does not know the problem or the brand, but everyone knows the category —
"you get the category familiarity for free" — so the ad creates "awareness, demand and urgency at the same
time". The condition: "there has to be a real argument underneath it — a believable reason why the entire
category gets this wrong, why that matters, and why your product was built differently." If it sounds like
trashing rivals to sell, "the whole thing falls apart."

**[TEST]** Sound, and old (the "common enemy" position). Its limit is her own condition: the argument must
be true. An invented category flaw is a false claim about competitors — against Meta's rules and the
holding's. The mattress line promises work actually done ("I priced every…"); it must have been done.

### 6.2 Price — "expensive compared to what?" (`mei-Dd4QtSbMnqs`, whole)

**[HER]** "That's expensive" is the best objection: the person "already wants to buy; they just need a
reason." Avoiding price "makes you look out of touch." Acknowledge it, then answer one question —
**expensive compared to what?** "Price isn't the real cost. Opportunity cost is." Examples: insurance
("expensive until the catastrophic event"); a $497 ads course against the thousands burned testing the wrong
thing; one multivitamin against five bottles. "Your job is to make not buying feel like the expensive
option." Those who still leave "were never your customer."

**[TEST]** Sound as a reframe when the comparison is honest arithmetic (the site's "the math" concept: a
price against the category's real cost). It becomes manipulation when the "cost of not buying" is invented
fear. Insurance as her example is hers; the holding does not sell conventional insurance.

### 6.3 What to change when results fall — her ranking (`mei-DdozG77RwW7`; same text `meyt-M-EpTpHY_Jk`)

| Tier | Move | Her reason |
|---|---|---|
| D | Copy a competitor's ad | You cannot see the ecosystem that made it win; you are months late |
| D | Optimise for hook rate, hold rate or CTR | "These metrics literally do not predict performance at scale" (§9) |
| C | Discount | It works, but "you just gave up margin instead of fixing what's actually broken"; fine in Q4 or a real seasonal promo |
| B | Change the landing page | Only "if there's evidence that something downstream is the bottleneck"; never while also testing creative, messaging and personas — "too many variables at once" |
| A | Change the offer and how value is framed | Guarantees, trials, price anchoring, framing — not a deeper discount. "A better offer can amplify demand, but it can't manufacture demand that isn't there" |
| S | Test a new angle or motivation | Opens new territory; must come from real signal |

"You can change a hundred things and still make performance worse if you're solving the wrong problem."

## 7. Hooks and holding attention

### 7.1 Six hook types (`meyt--E0iBvhD4AI`, 2023, whole)

She places hooks inside AIDA (attention, interest, desire, action). "I can guarantee you all hooks fall into
one of these six":

| Type | What it is | Her example and upgrade |
|---|---|---|
| Shocking or extreme | The more extreme the opinion, the higher the hook rate | MrBeast on an Antarctic mountain; upgrade: put the stake in the words ("I risked my life climbing a frozen mountain for a chocolate bar") |
| Relatable statement | A shared experience that qualifies the audience — only those it applies to stop | "I've tried everything but I just can't scale my Facebook ads"; rules: be specific, name a shared experience, press the biggest pain |
| Question | The brain cannot help answering | "Looking to renovate your home?"; upgrade: stronger word ("ultimate", not "best") and an authority title on screen |
| Pattern interrupt | Something the feed never shows: unusual visual, contrast colours, extreme close-up, an object used wrongly | Split screens; a Twinkie as a hair curler |
| Negative hook | A negative opinion that turns positive | "I don't like [brand]… now I have a reason to stop wasting my time at the nail salon" (full method, §8.6) |
| Declaration | A bold claim | Her least favourite — "TikTok made me buy it" is worn out; push it or skip it |

Her standing upgrades when she tears an ad down: start on the clip with the fastest movement; contrast the
two halves of a split; add a credential; replace weak superlatives.

### 7.2 Keeping them (`meyt-yorjIXycq-8`, 2023)

**[HER]** Sell the outcome and the feeling, never the feature ("forget the specs… sell the feeling of
gliding down the mountain in a cool jacket"). Text overlays always — "half of all users scroll without
sound" (her figure); the offer in text at the start or throughout, never only at the end. Quick cuts and
zooms; fast speech for the young, slower pacing for over-45s. Vary shots and angles. The routine format: the
product woven into a day (wake, teeth, breakfast, the supplement) — "when customers associate your product
with their daily habits they are more likely to purchase"; run through the creator's own account where
possible.

**[TEST]** Her 2023 position — "if there's one thing you should absolutely be optimising for it's your hold
rate" (`meyt-yorjIXycq-8 @01:00`) and "aim for a hook rate of at least 30%" (`meyt-z6ngfCm-6OI @15:10`) —
is reversed by her 2026 position (§9). Use the hook types as a vocabulary, not a target. "More frame changes
is always better" is contradicted by her own best current format: one line of text and a sound (§8.1).

## 8. The format library — the "mechanics"

Each is a container. By §4, changing the container is one legitimate way to iterate; the angle still comes
from research. "Trends die, frameworks don't" (`mei-DNjFVKnJQ4T @03:36`).

### 8.1 Text-only video with matching audio (`mei-DSGHOztkasS`, Dec 2025)

**[HER]** Words on a screen, no voiceover, sometimes nine seconds long. Her figures: 40, 31, 30, 24 and 4.7
ROAS on such ads. Where "97% of brands" fail: a POV line over random upbeat royalty-free music. **"The audio
you choose is everything. Your audio has to match the emotion you want customers to feel."** Find "the
emotional transformation your product delivers and then find an audio that matches that." The copy is the
customer's inner monologue, micro-specific (§2). It works because it looks like the organic posts around it.

**[TEST]** The principle is sound. Two cautions: trending sounds are not licensed for ads — Meta's and
TikTok's commercial sound libraries are the lawful source, so "a trending sound" in a paid ad needs the
commercial library or original audio; and ROAS of 30-40 on a small ad is typical of a closer catching warm
demand (her own §1 warning applies to her own receipts). "Humans process sound faster than images" is
broadly right for simple reaction time.

### 8.2 Notes-app ad (`mei-DNEPIv3Jiun`)

**[HER]** An ad that looks like a phone note. It works by familiarity and the "nosy person phenomenon" — it
reads like a private reminder or a friend's secret. "At least five versions running at all times." The copy
is everything: a headline that shocks or stirs curiosity and sounds like the customer. Her prompt, verbatim:

> "Write casual conversational copy for [product or service] that feels like someone's personal thoughts in
> their notes app. The tone should sound relatable, informal, and maybe a little witty or self-aware. It
> should capture real-life frustrations or desires in a short punchy human way. Headlines should be no more
> than 10 to 12 words; add two to three short bullet points under each line to reinforce the main idea with
> key benefits or features. Keep the bullets just as conversational and to the point; avoid sounding like
> traditional ads. This should sound like an authentic unfiltered thought."

Test: light mode against dark mode ("lately dark mode has been outperforming"); with and without an image
under the text; a long-form block of text against the short bulleted one.

### 8.3 Email-styled ad (`mei-DNjFVKnJQ4T`, Aug 2025)

**[HER]** An ad made to look like an email. Her figures: 10.8 and 17.5 ROAS; $21k spent for nearly $110k;
$20k for nearly $82k; 12.3 and 3.8 in another account. It "feels like reading something private", and the
format is familiar. Sender: a founder or CEO, or "someone unaffiliated — a customer or persona that
customers can identify with". Works as an evergreen ad, not only for sales. Her two prompts are shown on
screen and are **not in the transcript** (gap, see OPEN-QUESTIONS).

**[TEST]** A founder email is honest. A "customer" email written by a model — she praises one that sounds
"like an actual review written by a real person" — is a fabricated testimonial (§11).

### 8.4 Post-it note (`mei-DKNcQDmpfXI`)

**[HER]** The product, a sticky note, a short handwritten line — a call to action, a benefit, the offer, a
bold claim. One to five notes. "Ugly ads" stand out among polished ones. **Hand-write it and photograph it
with a phone**: "people can smell fakeness from a mile away" — typed text on a picture of a note defeats the
purpose. She says it works as well or better for service businesses, and as a video of notes only.

### 8.5 Testimonial static (`mei-DKsWXqIpW_k`)

**[HER]** An image plus one customer quote. Always running. The quote must be **specific** — not "I love
this" but (her supplement example) "I can finally poop regularly now". At most three lines, in quotation
marks, with the customer's name; the five stars are optional. In ad copy: paste a whole review with five
star emojis, quotation marks, first name and last initial; long reviews work "phenomenally"
(`meyt-KRjua8xK-9k @01:00`).

### 8.6 Negative-hook video, done with sarcasm (`mei-DLIqmi-pUpR`)

**[HER]** Open with a complaint: "This is the worst ___. Here's why." · "These are worse than [current
solution]." · "I absolutely regret trying ___." · "You could never make me try ___." · "POV: ___ is the worst
purchase I've made in a long time." The usual failure: the negative opening is followed by plain praise, so
the video makes no sense. Done right, every complaint is a humble-brag and quietly lowers every other
product: "It actually gaslit me into thinking all the other foundations I've used were a perfect match" ·
"people keep asking if I'm wearing makeup… as an introvert I didn't sign up for this level of attention" ·
"now I can't cheap out on drugstore makeup" · close on curiosity: "what is in this stuff?" Her prompt,
verbatim:

> "Write an ad script that starts with a bold negative hook about the product, service or brand being
> promoted. Use an attention-grabbing line that sounds like a genuine complaint, similar to the examples
> below. The hook should feel dramatic or sarcastic, grabbing attention and creating curiosity. Quickly flip
> the narrative into a positive, highlighting how the product, service or brand unexpectedly solves a major
> pain point or delivers outstanding results. Use a snarky, humorous and relatable tone, blending hyperbole
> with authentic benefits to make the messaging feel personal and engaging. Keep the structure flexible so it
> works across all industries, emphasizing how to turn a perceived negative into a powerful selling point."

**[TEST]** The structure is good writing. Her acne demo ("in just two days it's like my skin got the memo…
I'm just exaggerating here, it could be whatever time frame") shows the trap: an invented result and time
frame in a first-person voice breaks Meta's unrealistic-outcomes rule and is a false testimonial.

### 8.7 POV hook (`mei-DKu54udpQpZ`)

**[HER]** Replace "If you're struggling with X you need Y" with "POV: you struggle with X and want Y". The
key: micro-specific situations taken from reviews, comments and Reddit. Examples: "POV: you spent $800 on
skincare but your skin still looks the same" · "POV: you're googling quick workouts for people who hate
working out" · "POV: adulting is when you get excited about buying a new trash can" (for any dull household
product). Every brand "can find at least five oddly specific scenarios" in its own or competitors' reviews;
paste them over existing footage for new ads.

### 8.8 Us-versus-them static (`mei-DMvl5lPJJh8`)

**[HER]** A simple comparison; strong for new brands; long-lived; a bottom-of-funnel staple. Statics fail
when they cram features, testimonials and proof into one asset — "the simplest statics perform best". The
rows of the comparison must be in the customer's words, from reviews.

### 8.9 Split screen (`mei-DKAlFFkJO5c`; `meyt--E0iBvhD4AI @08:55`)

**[HER]** Two videos side by side, or a static beside a video; as a hook or a whole ad. Variants: problem
against solution; two contrasting clips (different colour and look, or the split is wasted); a product shot
beside a demo; an offer graphic beside a video. **Before-and-after in health, weight loss, fitness,
supplements, money:** label it as time passing — "month one vs month five" — never "before / after"; she
says wrong before-and-afters cost clients their accounts and "millions".

**[TEST]** The relabelling is not a safe harbour. Meta's Personal Health and Appearance standard marks a
before-and-after weight-loss image itself as non-compliant, and its Unrealistic Outcomes standard bars
exaggerated results, whatever the caption (transparency.meta.com/policies/ad-standards — "personal health
and appearance", "unrealistic outcomes"). Her trick lowers rejections; it does not make the content allowed.
The sound version for restricted categories: show the product in use and the routine, not a body compared
with itself.

### 8.10 Moving grid (`mei-DK0EEkppOPS`)

**[HER]** Four videos in a 2×2 or nine in a 3×3, playing together; "we've run them on every account"; made
in Canva by copy and paste; also usable as a hook that cuts to full screen. Rules: at most **three main
colours** across the whole frame (monochrome is safest); every clip the **same length**, so none ends on a
black tile; each clip **slow and continuous**, no jump cuts or zooms.

**[TEST]** The rules are good craft. "The human eye is only capable of processing three colours at a time"
is false as stated; the working rule (limit the palette) stands.

### 8.11 Comment-reply ad (`mei-DeKLEO3sNsn`, Oct 2026)

**[HER]** "The easiest ads to make right now." Her figures: 3.6, 5.7, 7.5, 7.3 and 11 ROAS; she says RYZE ran
one over 80 days and HexClad over 100. Not only for retargeting. Do not use the FAQ as the comment — "that's
how your creative dies in a week". Use the audience's inner monologue. **Answer the comment in the first
three seconds.** The rest must: force identity recognition, reframe the belief, hit **one** value
proposition, carry emotional triggers, deliver the payoff. She uses a tool that generates native-looking
comment bubbles for TikTok, Instagram, Reddit and text messages: "anyone can generate a fake comment in 30
seconds."

**[TEST]** The structure — one real objection, answered at once — is strong. The generated comment is the
problem: a bubble with an invented handle presents a made-up person's words as real (§11).

### 8.12 Listicles, and the Q4 list

**[HER]** A caption asks "are you running listicles?" (2025-05-07) and §4 names the listicle as a mechanic;
no reel in the set teaches it. Her Black Friday format list, in full (`mei-DeHlzeXsOVL`): offer-forward

strike-through price · reply to comments · tweet · apology note · value stack · "do not buy this" · sticky

**[ADDED]** What the list has in common: each borrows a surface people already read privately or trust

### 8.13 Older advice still usable

- Statics with an animated sticker exported as a GIF for cheap variety (`meyt-z6ngfCm-6OI @11:50`).

## 9. Reading an account

### 9.1 Hook and hold rate are diagnostics, not targets (2026)

**[HER]** "I've analyzed hundreds of ads and these metrics literally do not predict performance at scale.
Some of the best-performing ads had worse metrics than the losers." They help "diagnose an ad early when
there's little spend behind it"; once an ad scales they worsen "because your ad successfully expanded beyond
the easiest audience. So optimizing for them is backwards. You can build an elite hook-and-hold-rate machine
that prints zero money." (`mei-DdozG77RwW7 @00:38-01:09`)

How she reads them (`mei-DVJqJuBEYnh`):

| Pattern | Her reading | Her remedy |
|---|---|---|
| Weak hooks | "You're not creating new demand, so Meta shoves you into more expensive auctions"; CAC climbs however good retargeting is | A new angle — not eighteen rephrasings of the hook |
| Strong hook, weak hold, rising CAC | "You made a trailer, not an ad"; the angle is clickbait — "eyeballs, not buyers" | Rebuild the value section (§5, four parts) |
| High spend, flat growth | "High spend does not mean you can scale. It just means you can afford to mail it in" | Depth: emotional discovery |

Formulas — hook rate = 3-second video plays ÷ impressions, made as a custom column in Ads Manager
(`meyt-z6ngfCm-6OI @14:20`). **[ADDED]** She never defines hold rate; the trade uses ThruPlays ÷ impressions
or 15-second plays ÷ 3-second plays — pick one and keep it.

**[TEST]** Her 2026 statement is the better one and is a claim from her own accounts ("hundreds of ads"),
unpublished. It matters for any pre-publish scorer of hooks or attention: by her experience such a score
ranks stopping power, not sales, and must be tested against sales before it gates anything.

### 9.2 The funnel-leak read (`mei-DU6SlHXEbaV`, Feb 2026)

"Even the intern knows CAC is high, but that doesn't tell you why." Read the steps in order; move on only
when a step is clean.

| Step | Metric | Her reading | Her healthy example (one account, $350 average order, $40 CAC) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Landing-page views ÷ link clicks | "Link clicks are not equal to traffic." At 50%, half the paid clicks never loaded the page — "a huge revenue leak that nobody checks" | Near 100% ("unicorn level"); link CTR about 1.62% |
| 2 | Add-to-cart rate | Did the page sell the product? Cheaper CACs go with higher add-to-cart rates | 7-17% |
| 3 | Initiate-checkout rate | Who commits. About 4% at a high price means demand is real; 2-3% is "a value or price perception issue" | 4% |
| 4 | Add-to-cart → purchase | A health check, not a main KPI. 10% would be "a trust problem" | 27% |
| 5 | Click → purchase | Aim for 0.5-2.5%, depending on price and intent | 2% |

"Don't obsess over industry averages. The only thing that matters is your AOV-to-CAC relationship."

Older rules of thumb, still usable as first questions (`meyt-KRjua8xK-9k @11:00-15:30`): a link CTR of at
least 1% in a sales campaign means the ad is doing its job; $100 spent on one ad with no add-to-cart points
at the page — check that page copy matches the ad, that there are product photos, video and reviews; a store
conversion rate of 3-5% is the aim. On low order values (`meyt--wiLagDYEGs @04:10-06:00`): with a $10-50 CPM,
a $20-30 order value yields about 2 ROAS at best — raise the order value (bundles, cross-sells, one-click
upsells — `meyt-10PeJe_V9v0 @25:30`) before trying to scale. A high CPM is a symptom: small audience,
competition, offer-audience mismatch, older age bands, season (`meyt-pYNe8PZwl2I`, whole).

**[TEST]** The order of reading is right and is the most reusable diagnostic in the set. The thresholds are
from single accounts; "industry average conversion rate of 10% for Facebook ads" (`meyt--wiLagDYEGs @05:10`)
is not a figure to repeat. Her frequency thresholds (1.5-2 healthy, rotate above 3, `@06:55`) are habits.

### 9.3 The 30-second account gut-check (`mei-DVtpBFKEWLI`, Mar 2026)

From a $90k-a-month account she inherited. Red flags:

- More than one campaign doing the same thing.
- **Creative naming** — "tells you everything you need to know about the thinking level."
- The agency "can't explain why each ad set exists in one sentence."
- 1% lookalikes in 2026 — "fossil behavior… a legacy tax on a deprecated algo that worked six years ago."
- Micro-budgets ($15, $75) and arbitrary bid caps ($345.32) — "tickling the algorithm".
- Eight versions of one ad — "no hypothesis, no creative strategy, no understanding of how Meta learns".
- Countries with different buyers in one ad set (US, UK, Turkey, Poland) — "geographic soup… Meta can't
  optimise for seven different profiles at once, which always means lowest-quality traffic".

Her claim: the account's "best month ever" 3 ROAS was beaten in one month in its slow season; the site's
case card for a "$90K/mo luxury travel account" shows 2.61 → 3.04 (+16%) in the first 30 days.

**[ADDED]** A naming convention is implied, never shown. One that carries her dimensions:
`<concept#>_<persona>_<stage>_<mechanic>_<angle>_<job>_<v#>_<date>` — so a report can be cut by any of them
and "why does this ad exist" is answered by its name.

### 9.4 Structure (2023, restated 2026)

Three campaigns, three to five ad sets each, at most five ads per ad set (`meyt-HZtWh6ddWxI @02:40`); one
campaign per audience type; audience overlap under 10% is healthy, above 20-40% consolidate
(`meyt-KRjua8xK-9k @03:50-06:30`). **[TEST]** The numbers are of their time (§12); the principle — few
campaigns, each with a stated reason — is Meta's "account simplification" and still current.

## 10. Seasonal — Black Friday and Q4 (`mei-DePQmvqM-xv`, Oct 2026)

**[HER]** Five mistakes:

1. The offer is not instantly visible. "If they can't see your offer from space, they're scrolling." In
   video nobody waits two seconds. Run **at least 80% statics**.
2. Not setting bid caps at **10 times CPA** — "the brands doing this are cooking you".
3. Testing anything new during the event — messaging, personas, formats. "Do all of your testing now and
   scale what works."
4. Killing evergreen ads. "Your best ad didn't stop working just because it's Black Friday."
5. Trying to fill the funnel with new people. "Black Friday is not for reaching new customers. It's for
   retargeting them."

Supporting data from 2022-23 (`meyt-z6ngfCm-6OI @12:10-13:30`): in one account in November, image ads 133
purchases against 78 for video, image carousels 156 — "customers are scouring the web for the best deal, so
present your offer in a static image rather than a 15-second video."

**[TEST]** Points 1, 3, 4 and 5 are consistent with §1 (the event is demand capture; creation happens in the
weeks before) and with her data. Point 2 is risky as written and sits oddly beside her mockery of arbitrary
bid caps (§9.3): a bid cap at ten times the target CPA tells the auction the advertiser may pay up to that
for a conversion — it wins more auctions in a crowded week, and it can also overpay heavily. She gives no
evidence. Treat it as untested; if ever tried, on a small budget with a hard daily limit. Changing agencies
or teams in Q4 is her other warning (`mei-DSVhKkvkbHK`).

## 11. Where her advice crosses a line — and the sound version

| Her advice | The problem | The sound version |
|---|---|---|
| "If you don't have any reviews, look at your competitors and paraphrase some of the best ones" — as a quoted, named testimonial (`mei-DKsWXqIpW_k @02:55`) | A fabricated testimonial. The US FTC rule on consumer reviews and testimonials (16 CFR 465, in force 21 Oct 2024) bars testimonials by someone who does not exist or had no real experience, with civil penalties. Deception — outside the holding's boundary | Competitors' reviews are research for the brand's **own** voice. A quoted testimonial is a real customer's, kept with its source. A brand with no reviews runs founder, us-vs-them, villain and demonstration formats until it has them |
| Generated comment bubbles with invented handles (`mei-DeKLEO3sNsn @01:22-01:41`) | An invented person's words shown as a real comment | Use a real comment (handle hidden or with permission), or label the bubble as the brand's own framing — "the question we get most" — without a fake identity. The mechanic survives intact: one real objection, answered in three seconds |
| An email "from a customer" written by a model; first-person "review" scripts with invented results and time frames (`mei-DNjFVKnJQ4T @02:55`, `mei-DLIqmi-pUpR @08:20`) | The same rule; plus Meta's unrealistic-outcomes standard | The email comes from the founder or the brand. First-person result claims come only from a real user's real experience. A scripted performer is dramatisation and may not present invented results as experience |
| "Month one vs month five" instead of "before / after" (`mei-DKAlFFkJO5c @02:31`) | Relabelling does not make a restricted comparison allowed (§8.9) | Show use, routine and proof that is not a body compared with itself |
| "We steal their ads constantly" (AG1; `mei-DKsWXqIpW_k @00:42`) | Contradicted by her own 2026 "copying is D tier" | Take the container (a format anyone may use); never the angle, wording or assets |
| Push sexual suggestion ("sex sells", `meyt--E0iBvhD4AI @05:40`); profanity as voice; the "poop emoji" trick | Outside the holding's content boundary; the profanity is her personal brand, not a method | Specificity and candour without it — her own best examples ("first fender made for pontoons") need none |
| Download a viral TikTok without its watermark through a third-party app (`meyt-HZtWh6ddWxI @06:40`) | Fine only for one's own video | Keep the original file; never another person's video |

**The consequence for AI-made ads, which she does not address.** Much of "UGC" is first-person testimony.
An AI-generated person saying "I used this and…" with no real use behind the words is a testimonial by
someone who does not exist — the case the FTC rule names. The studio's own rule already covers it (B43 R39:
first-person testimony only when the use is real and recorded with the job). Outside that, AI-made people
present, demonstrate, dramatise and speak as the brand. Her faceless formats (§8.1-8.4, 8.8-8.10, 8.12) carry no such problem and are where a studio
without real customers on camera is strongest.

Her own remark points the same way: in sensitive categories "you can make ads that are incredibly resonant
and still eat s— because how you said it gets your distribution throttled… the difference between 'this is
what my customer wants to hear' and 'this is how I communicate that in a way that actually scales on Meta'"
(`mei-DdrX2NKB6_u @01:20-01:42`). She names the skill and does not teach it.

## 13. Her view of AI, agencies and learning

- **AI.** March 2026: she feeds Claude, Gemini, ChatGPT and Grok "winning ads, losing ads, performance data…
  they still can't do what I just showed you" (`mei-DWEt_ieEQKG @01:20-01:36`). October 2026: "you don't even
  need to know how to write ad scripts anymore" — the weekly Muse-based strategist writes the roadmap and the
  briefs (`mei-Dd_4CMkM-1v`). The site now sells "an agentic workflow": always-on listening, "agents that turn
  winners into the next round of briefs", automation to raise output. Her position moved in seven months. What
  she still keeps for the human: knowing **why** an ad won, and what to do when it fails.
- **Volume against quality.** "Most brands overcompensate for s— creative by drowning themselves in volume"
  (`mei-DSqEedWkZoS @01:01`). Yet the site's case cards say "up to 30 new creatives a week". Both are true in
  her system: many assets, each with a reason on the roadmap.
- **Learning ads in 48 hours** (her reply to Alex Hormozi, `mei-DdrX2NKB6_u`): setting up campaigns is
  trivial; "knowing what to run, why you're running it, and what to do when it doesn't work only comes from
  thousands of hours of pattern recognition". Metrics will not tell you "that your lander is on fire, you're
  not reaching cold traffic, or your messaging is garbage."
- **Agencies** (`mei-DSVhKkvkbHK`, `mei-DVtpBFKEWLI`): big agencies "move at the speed of a glacier… client
  number 37, 'we'll add this to the January sprint'"; "a 100-page deck of pretty slides and CAC still on

  accountability". Her scope lesson: asked for 200+ videos re-exported without text overlays after being
