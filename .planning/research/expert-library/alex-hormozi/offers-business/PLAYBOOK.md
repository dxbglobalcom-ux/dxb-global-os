## 1. The offer

### 1.1 The value equation `[fast 02:02–06:02, 10:04–11:04]`, `[13y 07:04–08:04]`

Value = (dream outcome × perceived likelihood of achievement) ÷ (time delay × effort and sacrifice).

| Lever | The buyer's question | How you move it |
|---|---|---|
| Dream outcome | "Is this exactly what I want?" | Sell the result and its status or money meaning, not the object ("it's not about the wood") |
| Perceived likelihood (the inverse of risk) | "How likely is it that I get it?" | Proof, guarantees, reputation — "you need the *perception* of the guarantee" |
| Time delay | "How long between paying and having?" | Deliver faster; give an early first win |
| Effort and sacrifice | "What must I do or give up?" | Done-for-you, white glove |

The perfect offer is "immediate, no effort, guaranteed, and exactly what they want". The buyer discounts
the outcome's worth by risk, delay and effort; your job is to shrink the discount. B2B is the easy case —
the outcome is money, so all the work goes into risk, speed and ease. B2C needs the status or pain
translated first. His father test `[fast 00:00–01:00]`: "if you paid a dollar and got five back, would you
do it?" — the father's three questions were likelihood, effort, delay. That is the equation.

**Rule: "You can't sell out of your wallet; you sell off the value you create."** `[fast 01:00]` The 2011
version: sell out of the buyer's *future* wallet.

### 1.2 Price is the strongest lever on profit `[fast 06:02–09:04]`, `[13y 56:21]`

Worked example (his): a table sold at $100 with $50 cost and $30 overhead nets $20. Repositioned at $1,000
with $250 cost (delivery added) and $130 overhead it nets $620 — a 10× price is a 31× profit. He says a
competitor who sold the same number of clients a month at the same CAC made about one-eighth the revenue
per client and about one-fiftieth the profit `[fast 08:03]`. His inference: **CAC inside an industry is
about the same for everyone competent; the difference is what each customer is worth.**

- "85% of companies competed on price and only 1% won on price" (he attributes it to McKinsey; **[test]**
  not verified — treat as rhetoric). Dan Kennedy: no prize for second-cheapest.
- A brand's strength is measured by how far above the commodity price it sells `[13y 56:21]`.

### 1.3 Who to sell to `[13y 00:01–08:04]`

"Sell to rich people until you have the money to sell to all the poor people. **The middle is where you
get killed.**"

- A buyer who pays 10% of his net worth expects the world; a rich buyer barely notices the same sum. Rich
  buyers pay more "in an absolute sense for lower relative value", which leaves margin to over-deliver.
- Volume at the bottom needs infrastructure and capital up front (Walmart, Amazon, Netflix; his Skool: "five
  years and tens of millions" before the $99 product was absurdly good).
- **The hack:** "do what somebody else is doing that sells to rich people, do it in half the time and
  charge twice the price." Rich buyers pay for time first, then certainty, then zero effort. "You won't
  sell a wealthy person on money saved; you'll sell them on time saved."
- The "zip code tax" `[event 05:03–06:04]`: the same lawn costs 2–3× more in a rich postcode.

### 1.4 Setting and raising price

- **Cost it out, then 5–10× the cost** `[event 04:02–05:03, 27:14]`. Split variable cost (scales with
  headcount) and fixed cost (a quarter of a planner who can run four jobs). Five is the floor "because it
  might go over". Where in the 5–10 range: "how rich is the person I'm talking to".
- **A pricing module, quoted live.** A sheet (he says AI can code it in hours) that returns the price while
  the client is on the call. "Never present an invoice via email" — the client will have questions and you
  want to be the one answering `[event 07:04]`. A quote that takes a week tells the client what working
  with you will be like; "first thing tomorrow morning" raises close rates `[event 21:14]`.
- **When the close rate is very high, raise the price.** An 85–90% close rate means "a lot of room"
  `[dental 10:07]`. Not raising prices for 3–4 years is a price cut. The practice raised 15–20% and close
  rate rose 3–4 points (the guest's report) `[dental 13:08–14:08]`.
- **When to stop:** "when conversion times price goes down" — not when you sell fewer. A 50% rise that
  loses 10% of buyers stays `[dental 28:11–29:12]`.
- **The virtuous cycle** `[dental 15:08]`: higher price → better customer → more over-delivery → more profit
  → better talent. Cosmetic and health services behave like Veblen goods: price reads as lower risk.
- **Prepay incentives.** 10% off for paying all up front; another 10% for committing to several jobs; if
  they break the commitment "just pay the difference" `[event 08:05, 29:15]`. Quarterly billing with a 5%
  "convenience" discount already baked into the price `[fast 21:14]`. Pay-up-front as the default, the
  payment plan as the downsell `[dental 11:08–12:08]`.
- **Speed is margin** `[roof 18:10–20:10]`: a 20% priority fee to go to the front of the queue — same work,
  reordered, more money; "if we miss it, we don't charge it". Use it whenever you are supply-constrained.

**[test]** The 5–10× rule assumes delivery cost is the scarce thing. Where delivery costs almost nothing
(our case) it gives a floor near zero and says nothing — section 1.1's value pricing is the rule that
survives. The "zip code tax" is honest when the price tracks scope or value and is the same for like
buyers; quoting by guessed wealth is not what we would do.

### 1.5 Guarantees

In these videos:

- **Price-lock guarantee** `[event 08:05, 27:14–28:14]`: "as long as you don't change anything (venue, date,
  headcount ±20%), this is the price." Offered as a choice: "nickel-and-dimed, or one price?" The padding
  is in the multiple.
- **Day-one money back** on a children's camp ("what if he doesn't like it?") `[park 09:06]`; a hard safety
  fact beside the button ("last year: zero falls") — trust goes next to the action.
- **Guaranteed speed or no fee** (the priority fee above).
- **Perception beats paper** `[13y 08:04]`: the best surgeon gives no guarantee; reputation and reviews do
  the job. "Proof over promise": 11,382 reviews at 4.7 beat any promise `[grow 15:09–17:12]`.

**[added — $100M Offers, the guarantees chapter, from my reading of the book; check the text before a
worker quotes it]** Four kinds: *unconditional* (refund, no questions — strongest, most refund risk);
*conditional* (refund or extra service **if the client did their part** — "do X, Y, Z and if you don't get
the result…"; he prefers a service remedy, "we work for free until you do", over cash back); *anti-guarantee*
("all sales final", used with a strong reason why); *implied / performance* (pay only on result — revenue
share, pay per lead). Rules: name the guarantee, state it as "if you do not get X in Y time, we will Z",
stack two or more, and expect sales to rise more than refunds — but **price the refunds in**: a guarantee
on a service with high delivery cost can sink you, which is why he pairs strong guarantees with conditions.

### 1.6 The grand-slam offer, bonuses, scarcity, urgency, naming

The videos use the term ("that was meant to be the grand slam offer" `[park 06:04]`) but never lay out the
construction. **[added — $100M Offers, from my reading; verify before quoting]**:

1. Pick the market: massive pain, purchasing power, easy to target, growing.
2. State the dream outcome.
3. List every problem and obstacle the buyer meets before, during and after — each through the four value
   levers ("it won't work for me", "it takes too long", "it's too hard").
4. Turn each problem into a solution ("how to … without …").
5. List delivery vehicles for each solution (one-to-one / group; done-for-you / done-with-you / DIY;
   live / recorded; speed; format).
6. Trim and stack: keep what is high value and low cost to deliver; drop high cost, low value.
7. Enhance with scarcity (a true limit on supply), urgency (a true deadline), bonuses (each named, each
   answering one objection, each with a stated value, presented one at a time after the core), guarantees,
   and a name.
8. Name it — the MAGIC formula: **M**agnetic reason why, **A**vatar, **G**oal, **I**nterval, **C**ontainer
   word (e.g. "Free six-week lean-by-summer challenge for busy mums").

What the videos add to this:

- **Always give the reason why** `[park 12:12]`: "early bird", "we have to ship it, so only now".
- **A bonus that is the thing they must buy anyway** `[sticker 15:08–17:10]`: give the physical kit (costs
  ~$10, valued at $149) only with the annual plan — "you'll have to buy this either way".
- **Anchor high, then come down** `[event 11:06, 29:15]`, `[park 20:22]`: quote the big number first so the
  real one "feels like nothing".
- **Say the same thing five ways and test which clicks** `[park 11:12–13:12]`: "buy 4 weeks get 6 free" =
  "buy one month, get the whole summer" = "as low as $200 a week".
- **Trade price for distribution** `[event 09:05–12:08]`: knock $5,000 off for a booth, another for a
  ten-minute talk, pay a fee per booking for one email to their list.
- **Unsell first** (the menu upsell, 2.3).

**[test]** Scarcity and urgency only hold for us if they are true (halal boundary: no deception). His own
rule agrees: "state the facts and tell the truth… change reality instead of exaggerating" `[grow 48:30]`.

## 2. Money models

His definition, in use: a money model is the sequence of offers that "pulls cash forward" so that one
customer pays for his own acquisition and delivery **and the next customer's** inside 30 days
`[park 24:27]`, `[fast 11:04–14:06, 27:17]`. Four stages: attraction → upsell → downsell → continuity.

### 2.1 Client-financed acquisition — the test every model must pass `[fast 11:04–22:15]`

> **30-day cash collected (gross profit) ≥ 2 × (CAC + COGS).**

His example: $100 to acquire, $50 to deliver → collect $300 inside 30 days. Then "capital is no longer the
constraint": every customer buys the next one. Why 30 days: it is the interest-free window of a credit
card; a 7-day cycle turns four times a month. He credits this for $1,000 → $10M in sales in ten months
(Gym Launch licensing) and for opening a gym every six months with $5,000 down `[fast 15:06–18:09]`.

Two ways to get there: raise the price, or get people to buy more **immediately**. "Ask to get paid
sooner": the ladder runs from the wage worker (paid after) through the contractor and the surgeon (paid
before) to the insurer `[fast 22:15]`. Industry terms such as net-60 are "their standard, not ours".

**[test — the holding's boundary]** The mechanism itself — customers prepaying — is interest-free and
clean. His *reason* for 30 days (credit-card float) and the practice's "pay by card or financing"
`[dental 11:08]` lean on interest-bearing credit: we neither borrow on a card to fund ads nor steer a client
into a lender's interest plan. The clean equivalents are in FOR-THE-HOLDING §2.4.

### 2.2 Attraction offers (get customers at break-even or better)

In the videos:

| Offer | How it ran | Source |
|---|---|---|
| **Paid challenge / paid trial** | A $10 five-day bootcamp; CAC $37; 22% buy the membership; AOV ≈ $36 — break-even on day one, profit by the end. His "wild test": sell the bootcamp as a **7-day trial of the membership** so 100% start on continuity, then push the annual during the week (he expects ~55% to stay vs 22% converting) | `[sticker 01:03–02:03, 18:10–21:10]` |
| **Giveaway** | Lead with the full-price prize ("$5,000 private summer giveaway"); 7-day countdown; one winner; non-winners get a "second prize" = a large discount with a 72-hour claim window; a referral link gives extra entries and the referrer wins too; the sales team then calls every lead. Result (guest's report): 3,700 local leads, summer camp revenue +325% on the prior year, and a sales team too small to call them | `[park 13:12–22:25, 25:29]` |
| **Buy X get Y free** | "Buy 4 weeks, get 6 free"; test the wording | `[park 11:12–13:12]` |
| **The cast-aside small job** | Lead with the repair "nobody will even show up for", raise its ticket by bundling, upsell after. Services sold went from 15–20 to 60–80 a month (guest's report) | `[roof 03:03–06:03]` |
| **Free work for proof** | "Before I enter any new space, I work for free in exchange for testimonials, reviews and feedback." Keeps bad word of mouth small and quiet while the product is weak | `[grow 16:10, 32:22]` |
| **Free tool / survey close** | "Want the slides? Scan the code" — the form asks "do you run events, how many a year" → a qualified lead list. His own: the free scaling roadmap (enter your numbers, get your stage) | `[event 10:06–11:06, 34:16]` |

**[added — $100M Money Models, from my reading of the book and third-party summaries; verify before
quoting]** The book's five attraction offers: *Win Your Money Back* (pay, do the stated actions, get it back
as cash or — better — credit toward the next purchase), *Giveaways* (above), *Decoy* (a plain version shown
beside the premium so the premium's value is seen), *Buy X Get Y Free*, *Pay Less Now or Pay More Later*
(a discount for paying today against paying full price later, often after a trial).

**[test]** "Everyone wins second prize" is a discount dressed as a draw. He himself corrects the guest for
*saying* everyone wins `[park 15:15–16:15]` — the fix he proposes is to hide it better, which is the wrong
direction for us. The honest form is in FOR-THE-HOLDING §2.4. Rules for prize draws differ by country; he
flags it himself ("run this by attorneys").

### 2.3 Upsells (more cash, sooner)

- **The hyper-buying cycle** `[fast 25:15–26:16]`: a new decision opens a window in which the buyer needs
  the whole kit (the marathon runner: shoes, then socks, shorts, a coach). Solving problem one reveals
  problem two. "Everyone's afraid to ask — just make the offer available."
- **Offer a different vehicle to the people who said no** `[fast 24:15]`: gym refusers bought more at a
  nutrition seminar than gym buyers did. Same problem, another way.
- **The menu upsell** — "my favourite chapter" `[roof 15:08–17:10]`. Four steps: (1) **unsell** what they
  do not need — this buys the trust; (2) **prescribe** what they do need; (3) **A or B?** — a choice
  between two ways to proceed; (4) **card on file** — "want me to put that on the card we have?" The roofer
  called it one of the most effective things he installed.
- **More of what they just bought** `[event 18:11–19:14]`: ask early "how many events a year do you run?"
  and bid for all of them — "we'll do the first; if it goes well, the other two at the same rate." A
  one-year client became a three-year client.
- **Order bump at checkout** — the thing they need to use the product `[sticker 15:08]`.
- **Priority/speed fee** (1.4).
- **Rebook at the debrief** `[event 22:14–23:14]`: within two days of delivery, a debrief call that ends
  with the next booking; a short video before it answers the after-sale questions.

**[added — Money Models]** The book's four: *classic* ("you can't have X without Y"), *menu* (above),
*anchor* (show the very expensive option first so the main one is a relief), *rollover* (credit what they
already paid toward the bigger thing).

**[test]** "A or B" is clean when both options are real. Asking for a card "to complete the patient
profile" `[dental 18:10]` is a pretext — ask for it openly.

### 2.4 Downsells

- **Payment plan as the downsell**, pay-in-full as the default `[dental 12:08]`.
- **Sell the annual only; offer the monthly after the cart closes, without the bonuses**
  `[sticker 17:10]`. Pushing the annual took its share from 5–15% to 30% and doubled average order value
  (guest's report) `[sticker 14:08]`.
- **Trial** as the softer first step (2.2).

**[added — Money Models]** *Payment-plan downsell* (same thing, split), *trial with penalty* (free or
cheap trial; the buyer pays if he does not do the agreed actions — which are the actions that make him
succeed), *feature downsell* (remove a feature — speed, guarantee, service level — for a lower price; never
simply discount the same thing).

### 2.5 Continuity

- **A membership bolted onto a one-time service** `[roof 12:05–14:06]`: $15–20 a month billed annually;
  "$2 of future repair credit for every $1"; includes the yearly inspection. "We don't even care about the
  money — we care that we have the appointment to see the person, and then sell more." Turns one-time
  revenue into recurring; "you might get 80% buying it" once timing and wording are right (the guest had
  not got it working at 90 days).
- **Permanent customers** `[fast 27:17–29:18, 32:22–37:31]`: count the cost of a customer who *never
  leaves*. If 1 trial in 3 converts and 1 customer in 3 stays, permanent CAC = 9 × the trial cost.
  Company A (sells 100, 200, 300 and loses all) and Company B (sells 100 a year and keeps them) both have
  300 customers in year three; only B has time on its side.
- **For an agency** `[fast 36:31–37:31]`: "an agency — a super high-churn business." Call a client of two
  years or more permanent. If one in ten becomes permanent, permanent CAC = 10 × CAC; then **find what the
  one looks like and advertise only to that avatar** — even at double the CAC, one in three is a win.
- **Recurring vs reoccurring** `[fast 35:29]`: a subscription, or a habit of buying (his Starbucks figure:
  $14,000 lifetime per customer).
- **Enterprise value** `[fast 28:18–32:22]`: recurring revenue is valued at a multiple, and that growth is
  untaxed until sale — "wealth alchemy". **[test]** His 10× revenue multiple is a software figure; an
  agency sells on profit at a far lower multiple. The direction holds, the number does not transfer.

**[added — Money Models]** *Continuity bonus* (join today, get a valuable thing free), *continuity
discount* (free months for prepaying or committing), *waived fee* (a setup fee waived if they commit to a
term; leave early and the fee is due).

### 2.6 Building one — his order `[sticker 05:05–22:11]`, `[park 05:04–20:22]`, `[fast 27:17]`

1. One attraction offer that breaks even on day one.
2. An upsell at the moment of purchase or onboarding.
3. A downsell for those who say no.
4. Continuity, and inside it the push to annual.
5. Check the 30-day rule. Only then spend more — "if a little works, more is usually better"
   `[event 13:10]`. Change one thing at a time and split-test at the same front price so the ads stay the
   same `[sticker 26:13]`.

## 3. Unit economics — his numbers and thresholds

| Measure | His rule | Source |
|---|---|---|
| "The core economic arbitrage of the business is LTV to CAC — we expand it and do it to infinity" | Track inputs (CAC, conversion, AOV, attach rate), not only outputs (revenue) | `[park 02:03]` |
| 30-day cash | ≥ 2 × (CAC + COGS) | `[fast 19:10–20:13]` |
| CAC ceiling | If LTV is ~$300 and you are profitable, spending $100 to acquire is fine; capping at $50 "for no good reason" is fear | `[sticker 05:05, 13:08]` |
| Price multiple on cost (service) | 5–10× | `[event 04:02]` |
| Close rate | 85–90% → underpriced; raise until conversion × price falls | `[dental 10:07, 28:11]` |
| Churn | 5% a month on a low-ticket membership is "really good" | `[sticker 28:14]` |
| Permanent customer | 2+ years; permanent CAC = CAC ÷ share who stay | `[fast 36:31]` |
| "We need cheaper leads" | Usually wrong: "they just don't make enough per customer because they're not that good" or are mispriced | `[13y 42:20]` |
| Rising CPL with flat CPM | Negative word of mouth, not the ad platform | `[grow 30:20–32:22]` |
| Launch leads | A third to a half arrive in the last 48 hours; model it | `[park 21:23]` |
| Volume before judgement | He tested 300 flyers; the mentor's *test* was 5,000 and the campaign 150,000. "What presents as volatility is a symptom of low volume" | `[grow 27:19–29:20]` |
| Speed to lead | Seconds, not hours — "4 to 5× sales"; on paid search "60 seconds", first responder usually wins | `[speed 11:05]`, `[dental 24:10]` |
| Booking delay | 15–30 days out → 7 days: close rate up, show rate +10 points (guest's report) | `[dental 26:11]` |

**[added — LTV:CAC thresholds, from a third-party summary of his podcast episode 985, "The Only Two
Numbers That Decide If Your Business Survives"; secondary source, not verified in his words]** LTV is
measured in **gross profit** (LTGP), yearly, back-of-napkin. The 3:1 rule of thumb is enough only when lead
generation, sales and delivery are all automated; with one of the three manual about 6:1; with two manual
at least 9:1; with none automated above 12:1. **[added — $100M Offers, from my reading]** Service gross
margin target 80% or more.

**[test]** The automation ladder is the single most useful number for us and it rests on a summary I could
not check against the episode. It is listed in OPEN-QUESTIONS.

## 4. The constraint method

### 4.1 The questions

- **Goal, current state, obstacle.** "If you can't clearly state current state, desired state, obstacle,
  you're running around like a chicken with your head cut off." His first two questions to any stuck owner:
  "What's the goal?" and "What problem are you solving right now?" `[13y 10:05]`
- **"Why can't we 10× this?"** `[13y 62:26–63:26]` Ask it of the main growth input (ad spend, outreach,
  sales heads) and read the answer: "ROAS drops when we spend more" → the ads; "we can't handle the calls"
  → hiring and training sales; "we couldn't deliver" → systematise delivery.
- **"If you doubled customers, does anything break?"** `[sticker 06:05]` No → demand-constrained. Yes →
  supply-constrained.
- **"What's the one thing that, if true, makes all the other problems go away?"** `[13y 47:21]`
- **"What, if it were true, would hurt your feelings the most?"** `[13y 82:33–84:35]` Usually the product:
  "have you been a customer of your own product?" "The big obvious thing is the problem, not the hundred
  other things."

### 4.2 The rules

- **You lack priorities, not information.** Strategy is the allocation of limited resources against
  unlimited options. The man with 56 companies; the media company with 40 million subscribers and no
  product `[13y 08:04–12:05]`.
- **The owner's favourite subject is rarely the constraint.** The sales lover asks about closing at a 40%
  close rate; marketers polish marketing. "The business is only as strong as its weakest link… the part of
  the bridge where only one dollar can get across" `[13y 12:05–15:05]`.
- **More marketing into a supply constraint does nothing.** "It does not increase throughput." He refused
  the dentist's content plan until capacity was fixed `[dental 31:12]`. And the reverse: do not push demand
  through thin margins — "fix the margins of the offer, then jam as much demand as we can" `[event 32:16]`.
- **Order when several things are wrong:** offer → price → sales motion → demand `[event 04:02]`.
- **Nail it before you scale it.** More locations is the wrong answer when one location is under-earning
  `[dental 23:10–24:10]`.
- **One bet when time is short** `[park 10:08]`: "you don't have the luxury of time… we need to make one
  bet." "A lot of things can work, but none will work if you try them all… you do more of fewer"
  `[event 35:18]`.
- **Leading, not lagging.** "Increase revenue" and "decrease churn" cannot be acted on. Masters hold more,
  finer metrics: his cold-outbound channel made one sale in four months while list → enrichment → pick-up
  → hook → offer → second-call show each got fixed in turn. "Heart surgery looks like murder to someone who
  doesn't know the milestones." Look for directional correctness `[grow 58:31–62:34]`.
- **Break the constraint into the smallest action and count it** `[13y 62:26–69:28]`: "we need salespeople"
  becomes "100 LinkedIn messages produce one hire; we lose one a quarter; to double in a quarter we send
  500 and hold 10–15 interviews a week." "If it is the constraint of the business, it is your job."
- **More, better, new** `[grow 23:16–25:17]`: more of what works is the best risk-adjusted move; changing a
  working thing "guarantees the cost of change, not the benefit". **70/20/10** `[grow 34:24]`: 70% an exact
  copy of what works, 20% adjacent, 10% new. (He credits Brin or Page; **[test]** the 70/20/10 rule is Eric
  Schmidt's.) "Boom" = an order-of-magnitude change (advertising) against a capped optimisation (a
  conversion rate cannot pass 100%) `[grow 00:01–01:01]`.
- **Better, not bigger** `[13y 36:17–43:20]`: each quarter, one sure improvement per function — and the
  next quarter **add** to it, never swap. The checklists that accumulate are "a hundred golden BBs, not one
  silver bullet".

## 6. His diagnosis method (how he fixes a stranger's business)

**Intake — the same five questions every time:** (1) Tell me about the business. (2) How do you make money
— the dollars: revenue, profit, ticket, CAC, conversion, AOV, LTV, churn. (3) How do you get customers?
(4) What do you think is stopping you? (5) Why does this matter to you (will the owner execute)?

**Then one or two probing questions that expose the constraint**, a plan of three to five moves in order,
a place on the roadmap, and a check after 90 days.

| Case | Owner's own diagnosis | The probe | His diagnosis | The moves, in order |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Roofing | "I need two salespeople and a project manager" | "How many doors per sale?" (20) → gross profit per repair vs replacement | A reliable way to get money already exists; the product mix is wrong | Specialise in repairs and raise their ticket; menu upsell on the onboarding call; two short sales videos; membership; speed fee; all-in on the one channel, then recruit door-knockers "as your new avatar" |
| Events | "I don't know which acquisition channel" | "How do the prices get made?" ("make-believe") | Mispriced first, then a sales-motion problem (owner converts 10× his reps), then demand | Cost sheet → 5–10× → live quote; price lock, prepay and multi-event discounts; trade discount for booth/talk/list at B2B events; daily sales huddles, reps become setters; three sales videos; the one best channel, more of it |
| Stickers | "I need more people in the bootcamp" | "If you doubled customers, does it break?" (no) "Why don't you spend to $100 CPA?" | Pure demand; the cap is the owner's fear and a thin first-30-day cash figure | Annual-only with a physical bonus; trial as the front end (split test); creative in hundreds from customers; pain-level hooks; spend cadence |
| Dental | "Three locations, one doctor" | "Which of the five visits must your father do?" | Supply-constrained on one expert; underpriced (85–90% close) | Associates take the routine visits; pay-up-front default; raise price; video before the money talk; cut booking delay; no new location, no new channel yet |
| Park | "Demand and cash" (14 days of cash) | "What's the monthly nut? What's the debt service?" | Survival: one cash-forward bet | The strongest offer to the existing list; giveaway to build leads; landing page cut to one message with proof at the button; phones; then repeat the play on birthdays and hire salespeople |

Patterns: he never adds a channel; he takes things away; he fixes the offer and its price before the
volume; he looks for the thing already working and multiplies it; where time is short he makes one bet.

## 7. Choosing the vehicle — the "idiot index" `[index, whole]`

Five classes by difficulty against money:

1. **Labour-heavy commodities (avoid):** low barrier, fierce competition, operational drag — the sit-down
   restaurant, the service gym, **"an SMB agency"**, retail, event planning. "How many billionaires came
   from it?"
2. **Easy, low money (starter cash):** drop-shipping, light home services, affiliate content.
3. **Expert-dependent (income without enterprise value):** coaching, **"AI implementation"**,
   **"enterprise-level agencies"**, surgeons, personality brands. High margins, hard to sell.
4. **Moat-built (hard, high money):** "true AI, not wrappers", franchising, roll-ups, marketplaces, SaaS
   ("getting easier with AI… this moat is beginning to erode"), consumer brands, regulated industries.
5. **Easy, high money:** demand far ahead of supply, often for a window — an ageing population (med spas,
   wealth advice, elder care), a rule change, and the **boring or unglamorous** ("it doesn't sound good at
   a cocktail party" — commercial cleaning, elevator maintenance): sticky, recurring, few entrants.
   Commercial cleaning "has some moats for AI".

"The difficulty can be a competitive advantage." And `[13y 86:36–88:38]`: his wealth came in four windows
where an arbitrage opened (underpriced Facebook ads at 30:1; a product so good word of mouth did most of a
"100:1") — "you back the truck up", then throttle to protect delivery.

**[test — the holding's boundary]** His class 4 and 5 lists include banks, interest-leveraged property
funds, debt collection, casinos and cannabis. Those are outside the holding; the *reasoning* (barriers,
supply against demand, stickiness, boring beats cool) is what transfers.
