## 1. His model of what a sale is

Everything else hangs on five statements.

1. **Sales is education over trust.** "Sales is passing conviction from one person to another; it's an education
   process over a bridge of trust." If the prospect knew everything the seller knows, they should buy. "If they
   knew everything that you know and wouldn't buy, and you get them to buy, it means that you deceived them."
   (UST 36:28–37:28) — CLEAN, and it is the test the holding should adopt word for word.
2. **Sales is personalised marketing.** A buyer needs a fixed set of things to be true in their head (say six).
   Marketing covers some; the seller finds which are missing for *this* person and covers only those. With
   perfect marketing no seller is needed (his examples: Tesla, Carvana). (UST 136:35–139:36)
3. **Trust and belief are continuums, not switches.** "They didn't trust you *enough*." A bigger purchase is a
   bigger plane and needs a longer runway. Three- and four-call closes mean too little education happened
   *before* the first call, so the fix is pre-call material, not better closing. (UST 139:36–141:37)
4. **A hundred golden BBs.** No single trick; about a hundred small things each raise the probability a little,
   and the great seller never skips one. "Good closers try to get hot; great closers never get cold." "Don't be
   cute" — two fat guys in the middle, run to the right. (UST 49:38, 32:25, 142:37–144:37)
5. **The prospect believes almost nothing you say and almost everything they say.** So the seller asks, and the
   prospect states the conclusion. "You don't close sales by being right, you close sales by making the prospect
   right." (3A 06:05, 22:10; UST 92:05)

His ethics, in his own words — these are the standard to hold his tactics against in §9:
- "State the facts and tell the truth." (3A 14:08; SF2 16:05)
- Bad selling is selling an unqualified prospect — "which basically means that you're lying to them that they're
  going to get a good result". (3A 14:08)
- "The moment that you feel a prospect is better served not doing something, you should stop." (UST 120:20)
- "Qualify prospects. Never lie." "There's nothing more powerful than learning to turn away prospects." (UST 126:25)
- "Don't try and pretend that automation is a real person. Everyone knows." (UST 16:11)

## 3. Before the conversation

**The sale starts at the click, not at the call** (UST 37:28). Every message between click and call frames
the firm.

- **A question can carry proof.** "Did you hear about us on the city's best boot-camp list?" — even when the
  answer is no, the prospect now knows about the list (UST 37:28). REPAIR: he adds that such lists are usually
  bought ("you usually just pay five grand to the magazine", UST 39:31). A bought placement presented as an
  earned award is hidden promotion. The device is CLEAN with an earned, checkable fact.
- **Edify the next person** (from Cialdini's *Influence*; captions garble the name). The setter tells the prospect
  who they will meet and why that person fits *their* case, with one or two matching results (UST 38:28–40:31).
  CLEAN when the results are real. The staged part — "making noise in the background… yep, he confirmed" — is
  CROSSES.
- **Qualify with BANT** — Budget, Authority, Need, Timing (he credits IBM; correct). Ask on the form, again in
  the setting call, and again in the closing call *before* price (UST 40:31).
- **Opening a call to someone who opted in** (local consumer leads, UST 43:32–45:35). Verbatim skeleton:
  "John?" (tone up, pause) — "It's Alex." (pause) — "I'm calling you because you opted in on Facebook for our
  promotion, and I just wanted to make sure that, one, you weren't a crazy person on the internet, and if there
  was a time that worked well for you to come in — and obviously to make sure you knew we weren't crazy people
  either." Then: "I've got 2:00 and 4:00 today, which works better?" Useful parts: three cues of what they opted
  in for (people forget within days), and two offered times instead of an open question. The tone that implies
  "you should know who I am" is REPAIR — identify first.
- **Cold opener**: voicemail or e-mail saying only "I'm calling about [their competitor]. Give me a call
  back." Then on the callback: "it looks like they're kicking your ass" or "we've reached out to them as well"
  (UST 41:32). His claim: the response rate "slays". CROSSES as taught (a pretext with nothing behind it);
  the repaired version in §9 is one of the strongest things in this file for a web office.
- **He used to pose as several employees** ("this is John… you're going to love Alex") and says so himself:
  "I was deceiving people and so that is wrong" (UST 45:35). CROSSES — and it is exactly the temptation an AI
  seller has.

## 4. The conversation — CLOSER, with discovery filled in

He calls it "an acronym that people remember", for one- and two-call sales (UST 57:42). For longer, larger sales
he says he uses a "diagnostic script" taught in another video — **not in my material**; I filled the discovery
part from his firm's own playbook (`DISCO`).

### 4.0 Open — proof, promise, plan (15–20 seconds)
The same opener he uses for videos (UST 49:38–52:39). Verbatim: "John, [we're here to] get going on this thing —
[the big promise]. We've done this with lots of other people who are just like you [proof], and so this is the
process that we're going to do, and at the end of the call we'll see if it makes sense to move forward. If not,
no sweat — I'm still going to send you on your way with stuff that could help you [plan]."
Why: an agenda controls the frame, shows the seller has done this before, and respects the buyer's time. A
banker's line he admired: "Can we just skip the foreplay?"

### 4.1 C — Clarify why they are here (UST 57:42)
Nobody takes a call "for more information"; they have a problem. Ask about **the action they took**:
"What made you reach out?" · "What made you hop on the call today?" · "What made you comment on my post / reply
to my e-mail / book on my calendar?"

### 4.2 L — Label the gap (UST 58:43)
"So just to make sure I'm clear: you are here, you want to get here, and this has been the issue that's been
holding you back." Present → desired → obstacle. "The shortest part of the script but incredibly important."

### 4.3 O — Overview the past: the pain cycle (UST 59:44–65:48)
"**The pain is the pitch.**" His reasoning: deprivation creates motivation, so the seller brings the deprivation
to the top of the mind. Quantify (a) what they have tried, (b) what it cost in money, time and effort, (c) the
effect on their standing, (d) the daily cost of not solving it — "the cost of inaction".
Core question: "What have you done so far to try to [goal]?" Everything they tried is an advantage: "they are
here in the sale, which means it did not solve the problem." For each past attempt, credit what it had and name
what it lacked — that lack is one of your pillars (§4.4).

**The nine steps of discovery** (`DISCO`, added; this is the detailed form of C-L-O):

| Step | Name | Question, verbatim |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Current | "Why did you reach out? Where are you now?" |
| 2 | Desired | "Outside of money, what are you trying to accomplish? What do you want to have happen?" |
| 3 | Obstacle | "What's your biggest roadblock right now? What's holding you back?" (the hidden question: "Why don't you have the results you want?") |
| 4 | Reason | "Why? Example?" |
| 5 | Pull teeth | To a vague, confusing or incomplete answer, always the same two: "I see. Can you tell me more about that?" then "Ok, cool. Can you give me an example?" |
| 6 | Recap | their problem in **their** words |
| 7 | Label | the problem in **our** words — one of a small fixed set of labels that map to the offer (his five: Marketing, Sales, Product/Delivery, People, Profit) |
| 8 | Confirm | "Does that sound about right?" |
| 9 | Repeat | "What else is holding you back? What's your next biggest problem?" — steps 3–8 again until enough solvable problems are labelled |

Then **stack the pain**: say all recaps again, label the whole, confirm. His example: "You tried three different
marketing agencies, spent $50K, got leads that didn't convert, and now you're hesitant to invest again. — So it
sounds like the real issue isn't just vendors, ads and leads; it's that you need an entirely new marketing and
sales process. — Does that sound right?"
Rule: "**We only care about problems we can solve**" — ask only about results the offer can deliver; "have a
list of results to ask about ready to go". Discovery is finished when the prospect agrees with the final recap.

**Kill zombies early** (UST 97:08–98:08). Whatever regularly blows up at the price — budget, authority, timing —
is asked during discovery, when "there are no stakes on the table". The past-attempts question collects them
naturally: what did that cost, when did you start, "was your husband / partner involved in that decision?".
Asked before price the seller is a fact-finder; asked after price, "it's a way to extract money". Sit "on the
same side of the table" — a guide, shoulder to shoulder.

**Shifting from local to global** (SF1 16:14–21:16). A buyer who knows one variable compares on that variable:
"Customers will always go to price if you don't give them any other variables to consider." Discovery questions
therefore also *teach the criteria*: "Do you care about the qualifications of the movers?" "Do you already have
the materials?" "Do you have anything valued over $500?" "Did the other guys give you a cap?" Each yes becomes a
line of the offer. "That's the point of a good sales process: 'I didn't even think about these things.'"
REPAIR: in the same passage he invents the statistics that make the criteria frightening — see §9.

### 4.4 S — Sell the vacation: the three-pillar pitch (UST 62:46–70:52)
- **Two minutes at most.** "You want to be talking about the prospect, not the product." Spend "90 % of my time
  trying to understand the pains".
- **Chunk the offer up to three pillars** — people think in threes; a seven-step process is grouped until it is
  three. His gym: fitness, nutrition, accountability ("if you have all three you can't fail"). His lead agency:
  leads that are timely, qualified, exclusive.
- **Each pillar = one statement + one metaphor**; no mechanics. "Closes die in the details."
- **Tie each pillar to what their past attempt lacked.** "You had fitness and nutrition, you didn't have
  accountability… all we have to do is fix this one thing."
- **Sell the destination** — "Maui, not the plane flight, not TSA". REPAIR for services whose flight is long
  (SEO): the buyer must hear the honest duration and effort once, before price.
- His metaphors, usable as they are: the three-legged stool (two legs balance for a while, then fall); the
  toothbrush (a parent was the outside accountability until the habit became your own); the Amazing Race back-seat
  local ("I've been where you're going, with a hundred others"); and for our trade: "**Think about your ads as
  your paycheck — you put work in, you get money out immediately. Think about SEO like an investment account —
  you put money in, it's not much for a while, but eventually it starts compounding.**" (UST 70:52). He continues
  "within one or two years it's going to be bigger than anything you could do actively" — that half is a promise
  nobody can keep; drop it.
- **The ask**: "Does that sound good? Ready to get started? Ready to get some traffic to your site?" then "Great,
  let me get your information." (UST 71:53)

### 4.5 E — Explain away concerns
Sections 5–7 below.

### 4.6 R — Reinforce the decision: the handshake (UST 108:16–110:19)
After payment, not a hand-off ("good luck") but a handshake: the seller introduces the delivery person by name,
with what that person will do for *this* goal; the delivery person opens with a proof-promise-plan built from the
seller's notes. **BAMFAM** — "book a meeting from a meeting": at every contact the customer knows when the next
one is and with whom, and that person already knows the whole conversation. His claim: fewer back-outs, cold
feet and first-30-day refunds. From SF2 (17:05): onboard the next day — "give them value as fast as you can… the
likelihood they refund is really low".

### 4.7 Delivery rules that apply through the whole call
- **Follow the script word for word up to the ask.** "If everyone follows the script and doesn't close, you
  have a script problem. If people aren't following the script you can't change anything." About 12 questions
  with one or two follow-ups each. After the ask the conversation branches (UST 52:39–53:40).
- **Tone is part of the script.** Emphasis (where you stress and pause), tone (down = statement, up = a request
  to answer), pace (slow = important; faster while approaching the close, slower and lower at the ask). Mark the
  script with underline / caps / italics / bold as a tone guide (UST 53:40–56:42).
- **The prospect talks two thirds of the time**; let silence sit, people expand when you do not answer
  (UST 141:37).
- **When the prospect says yes, stop selling.** "Do not sell yourself out of a sale" — new details can turn yes
  into maybe (UST 73:55).
- **Never ask "do you have any questions?"** — "the worst question ever asked in sales… asking them to take the
  wheel" (3A 07:05). REPAIR in §9: the buyer must still be able to ask anything.
- **Train back to front**: first how to take payment, then how to ask for it, then the rest — without the first
  two the close rate is zero (UST 72:54).

## 5. When the answer is not yes — the method

"Closing begins once someone says no." (UST 72:54)

### 5.1 The 3A / AAA reframe (3A whole; UST 88:03–91:04)
"The one to three sentences you say after a prospect says anything but yes." Its purpose is arithmetic: "He who
asks the most times gets the most sales", but asking badly loses rapport, so every ask is wrapped.

1. **Acknowledge** — say their words back ("So you're curious what our certifications are"). Buys thinking time
   and shows listening. "When in doubt, say it back." (UST 99:09)
2. **Associate** — attach what they said to something positive: (a) a neutral compliment ("that's a smart
   question", "totally understandable"); (b) the behaviour of the best customers ("our biggest success stories
   think about implementation the way you are right now"); or, before a hard truth, a **foil**: (c) "I had
   someone earlier today who asked the same thing, can I share what I told them?", (d) a past customer like them
   ("that makes you just like Sarah, who had a similar plumbing business"), (e) an authority inside the firm
   ("our senior engineer told me this today").
3. **Ask** — the next question, which moves the sale on.

His five rules (3A): (1) they believe what *they* say — lead them to say it; (2) nobody can disagree with a
question — "be like smoke"; never voice disagreement, "you never win a sale by winning an argument"; (3) tell
them what their question means — it is what your best buyers ask; (4) use a straw man / foil for hard truths, so
the harsh sentence lands on a third person; (5) keep childlike curiosity — say "huh, that's interesting", seek to
understand, "keep the human number one".
Marks: Acknowledge and Ask are CLEAN. Associate (a) is CLEAN when meant; (b) and (d) REPAIR — only when it is
true of a real customer; (c) and (e) CROSSES when the conversation did not happen (he does not ask that it did).
The label trick — praise a "family man" early to say later that a family man would buy (3A 23:11) — CROSSES.

### 5.2 The first questions after any hesitation (UST 75:56–79:57; 3A 06:05, 10:07, 24:11)
In order of depth:
1. "What's your main concern?" (first no, early)
2. "What are the main variables you're using to make the decision?"
3. "What would it take for this to be a yes?" / "What makes this a yes?" — if they cannot say: "Can I share some
   of the decision-making variables I often hear?"
4. "What makes this a no?"
5. Late in the sale: "What are you afraid of having happen?" — "my universal response… it gets you to the heart
   of the issue as fast as possible".

### 5.3 Details are death traps (UST 81:59–88:03, 95:07–96:08)
A direct answer to a detail question is a "blind answer": the prospect judges it and you do not know which answer
they wanted. His decision tree:
1. Ask a question about the question: "Which certifications are you looking for specifically?" "How many days
   did you need it to be open?"
2. If their wanted answer is one you have: "That's exactly what we do."
3. If not: ask why they want it, chunk up to the reason, and ask: "If we got you to the same result either way,
   would you care how we got there?"
4. If you must answer: guess the reason aloud and answer the reason — "It sounds like you want to make sure
   you're safe. We haven't had a single injury in two years. Does that make you feel better?"
5. If pressed, the **secretary / mechanic** exit: "It's like asking the secretary at the heart surgeon's what's
   wrong with your heart"; "I can't ethically answer before we've brought your car into the shop and looked under
   the hood… and if anyone gives you an answer before looking, run the other way, because they're just trying to
   sell you stuff."
Underlying belief: a detail objection is a **preference** objection — "I want your result, my way". Answer:
"You change the variables, you change the outcome… what you have been doing hasn't been working for you."
Mark: steps 1 and 4 CLEAN (they find the real need). Step 2 as he describes it — "they told me what they wanted
and I told them that's what it was" — and using step 5 to avoid a question the seller *can* answer: CROSSES.

### 5.4 The understanding behind each objection family (UST 104:10–106:15)
"Don't memorise, understand." Keep two or three answers per family in your own words.

| Family | What it really is | Line of argument |
|---|---|---|
| **Price** | Value, never price: "nothing is ever too expensive, it's just not worth it" | Raise value; resourcefulness over resources ("a Ferrari for five grand — you'd find five grand") |
| **Time — "I'm busy"** | Priorities | What is more important than this, and does it return more? |
| **Stall — "let me think", "give me a week"** | Decision avoidance: fear of a wrong decision, or not knowing how to decide | Reassure; walk them through how to decide and which questions to ask |
| **Decision maker** | No authority — or a cover for the real concern | Find the concern; "support, not permission" |
| **Detail / preference** | "Your result, my way" | Change the variables, change the outcome |

### 5.5 All-purpose closes (UST 79:57–81:59, 107:16–108:16)
- **Zoom out — best case / worst case**: "Whoa, I feel like we've gone to Pluto and back. Big picture: best case
  we get you [result]; worst case you get six weeks of service and your money back." With a guarantee: "You have
  two options, both risk-free. Go home — guaranteed to leave you exactly where you are — or take a shot." CLEAN
  when the guarantee is real.
- **The 1-to-10**: "On a scale from 1 to 10, where are we?" → "What would make it a 10?"; to a six: "Why not a
  one?" so *they* list the good. "Are we feeling ten-ish?" CLEAN.
- **"The reason"**: turn any objection into the cause of their situation — "Do you think the fact that you've
  struggled to make decisions in the past is why you're on the phone with me today?" CROSSES — it converts a
  question into a character flaw.
- **Before a hard truth, get permission**: "Can I be a coach instead of a friend here?" "Would it be totally
  overstepping if I shared something that might be uncomfortable?" A young seller facing a senior owner uses a
  foil instead (UST 100:09). The permission question is CLEAN.
- **Stack closes**: on a pause, add a second and third angle on the same obstacle instead of waiting (UST 101:09).
  REPAIR — bounded in §9.
- **Objections are not always a no** (UST 148:38): "Wow, that's expensive" is an observation. Answer "Yeah,
  totally" and continue. "Unless someone says they don't want to buy, don't assume it." CLEAN and important.

## 7. Price presentation

1. **Start with the top tier, to everybody.** "I always start high. When you get on the phone with somebody who
   has money, they don't care, they just want it done — you have to give that person the opportunity to buy." On
   "I'm on a budget": "No worries, then just do standard." Keep raising the top tier "until people say no; you
   can always fall back on standard". (SF1 12:07–13:07, 26:16)
2. **Get out of the commodity unit.** "If a customer can look at two things and say these are the same, they will
   always take the cheaper." Sell by the job, not the hour (SF1 05:07, 40:23). For him this is the first chapter of
   *$100M Offers* (the offer slice owns it).
3. **Itemise the components** along speed, risk and ease — trained people, materials, insurance, a cap — and say
   them aloud. "They need to know they're getting way less for less." (SF1 10:07–19:14)
4. **A cap**: "It will not be more than this" (estimate plus a buffer), "unless there's a huge issue when we
   arrive on site". Then ask "did the other guys give you a cap?" (SF1 15:14)
5. **Charge for speed**: a same-day surcharge of 10 %, moving to 20 % — "speed is one of the biggest things
   people pay for" (SF1 13:07–14:07).
6. **Never discount to close; change terms or scope** (§6, O5).
7. **Price the owner's time honestly.** Checkout price $2,500; with a one-to-one call with the owner $3,000 —
   "state the fact and tell the truth: I can't physically talk to everyone". If everyone takes the dearer one,
   the price is too low (SF2 12:05–16:05).
8. **A reason to act now that is real**: a lower price and a bonus for buying during the live session (SF2
   17:05–18:06). REPAIR: he adds "you can say 'I'm not supposed to do this but I'll give you the bonus'" (SF2
   22:07) — a staged exception; CROSSES.
9. **Tier ratios must make sense**: one-to-one support priced at twice the group price was "not priced right…
   it should be like five times" (SF2 18:06).
10. **Sell the next thing mid-flight**: ask at the half-way point, at two thirds and at the end. Selling only at
    the end gets "about a third"; adding the earlier asks "half or more". "Sell when they're mid-decision, not
    making a new decision." (SF2 10:05–12:05). He also moves the programme to six-month automatic renewal,
    "opt out rather than opt in", while saying "I don't want to bug people without them knowing" — REPAIR:
    renewal terms stated at purchase and a reminder before each charge.

## 8. Follow-up, and how he diagnoses a failing sales process

### 8.1 Follow-up
- **The most important follow-up is the first** — its speed (M2). Then "personal, fast, multiple times over
  multiple days" (UST 05:05).
- **Reminders**: 24 h / morning / 1 h; iMessage, voice memo or video beat plain SMS because "people still don't
  entirely believe it's a real person" (UST 16:11–18:14).
- **Ghosted**: "just send them a meme" — the Kevin Hart "where you at"; his claim: the highest response rate of
  any message; "send it to 200 and you'll probably get five to ten that re-engage" (UST 71:53).
- **No for now is not no forever**: a pulse check every three to six months, without shame — some of their
  reasons were real (a merger, a better use of money) (UST 144:37).
- **Record everything; study the hot streaks**: when the seller is closing everyone, the tone, opener and
  transition are measurably different — find the difference and make it a checklist (UST 32:25).
- **Sales and delivery review recordings together weekly**: delivery hears what was promised; sales sees an
  onboarding for the first time and gains real conviction; over-promising stops; 5–10 % back-outs shrink
  (UST 28:18–31:22).
- **Conviction comes from results**: his closers' best day was weigh-out day, when they had just watched twenty
  people show results. In a slump, read testimonials before drilling scripts (UST 34:26–36:28).

### 8.2 The diagnosis — "back to front" (SF1 05:07; SF2 06:03)
Order of repair: **the offer → how it is sold → who it is sold to → how they find out.** In both episodes the
owner arrives saying "I need more leads / more hands" and leaves with an offer and a conversion fix.

| What the owner said | His diagnosis | His prescription |

Price is all they talk about because no other variable was given; the offer is a commodity sold by the hour | Two tiers, VIP first; itemised components (trained "master movers" with a stated threshold of moves, materials, insurance, cap); preference questions in the call; step down only on "budget"; same-day surcharge |

Then go one level up: one brokerage = 20 agents ≈ 60 jobs a year; visit in person, ask to be the only mover in their client packet ("you want integration") |
| SF1: 12,000 agent phone numbers, generic SMS | Same text to all gets flagged as spam and ignored | An AI reads each agent's public profile, drafts a personal first message per row in a sheet, a human skims and sends; test against the generic opener |

## 9. Where his tactics cross the holding's boundary — and the clean version

The boundary: no deception (no fake reviews, fake accounts, hidden promotion, invented facts or people) and no
pressure tricks. His own stated ethics (§1) agree with it; several of his taught moves do not. The idea survives
in every row.
