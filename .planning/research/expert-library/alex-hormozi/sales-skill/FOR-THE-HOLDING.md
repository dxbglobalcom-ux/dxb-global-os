# Alex Hormozi, sales skill — what the holding does with it

Companion to `PLAYBOOK.md` (same folder; § numbers and tags `UST`, `3A`, `SF1`, `SF2`, `DISCO` point there).
Lines served: **B84** DXB Web Studio and its client-finding leg, **B28/B43** the agency seat and ads, **B83**
revenue lines, **B64** the client record.

**The answer in one paragraph.** Hormozi's own finding is that what happens *around* the conversation — answering
in under a minute, seven days a week, prepared, never making the buyer repeat themselves, following one script
word for word — outweighs anything said in it. Every one of those is hard for a human team and native to an AI
worker. So the holding's seller is not a weaker copy of his closer; it is the seller his method describes and
his teams struggle to be. What the AI worker must *not* copy is the layer of staged scarcity, invented third
parties, invented statistics and relationship pressure (PLAYBOOK §9); removing it costs little, because for a
web office the honest substitute — a real measurement of the prospect's own site — is stronger than any of
them. Nothing below is built or tested yet; every number is his claim until we measure ours.

## 1. The ideas, ranked by value

### 1. The measured brief replaces every trick — "look under the hood" before the first word
- **What.** His "five minutes of prep makes you look like a genius" (UST 46:35), his 24-hour reminder ("we found
  three things we can immediately help you with", UST 16:11), his mechanic line ("I can't ethically answer before
  looking under the hood", UST 82:59) and his competitor opener (UST 41:32) are one idea: arrive with facts about
  *them*. A human does it rarely; our worker can do it for every lead.
- **Where.** B84 client-finding and every inbound lead; B28 for ad accounts.
- **Form.** A skill (`sales-prospect-brief`) producing a one-page brief from tools the office already decided on
  (Screaming Frog, DataForSEO, OpenSEO, Search Console where access is given): load time on a phone, indexable
  pages, what the search result shows, whether AI answers name the firm, the same three numbers for one named
  local rival, the contact path (is there a form, does anything reply).
- **What we build.** The brief template, three "findings" each stated as *fact → what a customer feels → what it
  costs*, and the honest competitor opener (PLAYBOOK §9 X17).
- **First test.** Twenty businesses in one niche and one city: ten receive the generic opener, ten the measured
  one. Count replies.
- **Earns / improves.** Reply rate and show rate; it is also the proof step of the call and the first page of the
  proposal.
- **Needs the CEO.** Approval of the outreach campaign (B84 already says an outreach campaign is his to approve).
- **Risk.** A wrong measurement in an opener destroys trust — every figure in a brief must come from a tool run,
  with the run kept.

### 2. Sixty seconds, seven days — the front desk
- **What.** PLAYBOOK §2 M1–M3. His claim: the largest multiplier in sales. The checked part: the HBR audit found
  most firms answer web leads in hours or days, or never.
- **Where.** Every form, chat, e-mail and (later) phone line of the holding's own site and brands.
- **Form.** A standing worker (`sales-first-touch`) + a measured number on the cockpit: median seconds to first
  human-quality reply.
- **What we build.** First reply that names what they asked for three ways (he: people forget what they opted in
  for), asks the two or three qualifying questions, and offers the two nearest real slots or "continue here now".
- **First test.** The holding's own site: every enquiry answered inside 60 seconds for 30 days; record reply
  time, qualified share, booked share.
- **Earns / improves.** The lead that today would cool overnight. It is also the alive-ness the CEO asks for: a
  surface where work visibly happens while nobody watches.
- **Needs the CEO.** Nothing for chat and e-mail. A phone number and outbound voice need an account and, per
  market, consent rules (OPEN-QUESTIONS).
- **Risk.** An instant reply that is generic is worse than a slower real one — the first reply must use what they
  wrote.

### 3. The front desk as a product — "your leads answered in a minute"
- **What.** The same worker, sold. Every small firm the web office talks to has M2's problem. Hormozi himself
  owned a company doing exactly this (lead nurture, "4,000 appointments a day"), and in SF2 he tells an owner to
  put an AI on the inbox and a suggested-answer dashboard on support (SF2 19:07, 26:11).
- **Where.** B84 monthly care package; B83 as a revenue line of its own.
- **Form.** A product: an AI front desk on the client's site and inbox, disclosed as AI, answering from the
  client's own service list and prices, booking into their calendar, handing over to the owner when it cannot
  answer. A monthly fee. (B84 already notes Google is offering selected businesses its own agent on Search and
  Maps; ours works on the client's site, e-mail and chat, and prepares the profile Google's agent reads.)
- **Free tool that feeds it.** A **response-time test**: the owner asks us to test their own contact path; we
  send one clearly labelled test enquiry and report how long the answer took. Opt-in only — a disguised enquiry
  would be a pretext.
- **First test.** The holding's own site is case zero (idea 2). Then one friendly business, free for 30 days,
  with before/after reply time and booked calls.
- **Earns.** Recurring revenue per client; the entry to the site and SEO sale.
- **Needs the CEO.** Price; a yes to selling it; the terms a client signs.
- **Risk.** A wrong answer in a client's name. Guard: answer only from an approved fact sheet; everything else is
  "I'll have the owner confirm".

### 4. One script, followed perfectly, improved weekly
- **What.** "I would rather have 100 % of my team saying the script word for word and close no one — because
  then I could change the script" (UST 52:39). With humans, adherence is the hard part. With an AI worker the
  script is the *only* variable: every loss is a script problem, every change is a clean test.
- **Where.** The sales seat; the improvement loop belongs with the office's review.
- **Form.** The skills in §3, plus a weekly `sales-tape-review`: every conversation is a transcript by nature
  (his "record every call" is free for us). The review reads the week's lost and won conversations, finds the
  stage where losses concentrate, and proposes **one** change (his "one piece of feedback at a time").
- **What we build.** A stage tag on every conversation turn; a loss-reason field with his five families; the
  review report. Delivery reads the same tapes (his sales + customer-success session) and flags any promise it
  cannot keep.
- **First test.** After the first 30 conversations, run the review by hand and see whether one stage stands out.
- **Improves.** Close rate over time; stops over-promising at its source.
- **Needs the CEO.** A script change that alters an offer or a price.
- **Risk.** Thirty conversations is thin; a change made on five losses is superstition (his own warning about
  "lucky socks", UST 32:25).

### 5. Discovery that teaches the buyer how to buy a web site
- **What.** The nine steps (`DISCO`) and "local to global" (SF1 20:16). A buyer who knows one variable compares
  price. Our clean form: discovery questions that hand the buyer *checkable* criteria — ownership of the domain
  and accounts, raw numbers visible, speed on a phone, a price cap, what happens when we part.
- **Where.** Every web, SEO and ads conversation; also a public page.
- **Form.** The question bank (§3.3) and a free **buyer's checklist** ("12 questions to ask any web agency —
  including us"), published ungated.
- **First test.** Publish the checklist on the holding's site; use it in the first ten conversations; note how
  often price is the first objection with and without it.
- **Earns.** Moves the comparison from price to criteria we choose to meet; the checklist is also content for
  search and AI answers.
- **Risk.** We must pass our own checklist, visibly.

### 6. The objection library, ranked by frequency, answered before it is asked
- **What.** "There are no new questions" (SF2 13:05). Bucket every question, rank by share, answer the top ones
  unasked. Fewer live objections, less pressure needed.
- **Form.** A data library (§3.5) with a count per entry, fed by the tape review; the top eight become the FAQ on
  each service page, the FAQ block of every proposal, and short videos (his "green screen" answers).
- **First test.** Seed with §3.5; after 30 conversations replace guesses with counts.
- **Improves.** Close rate, and search/AI visibility (real questions in the buyer's words).

### 7. Price: top tier first, itemised, capped, never discounted
- **What.** PLAYBOOK §7. A web site is sold by the job with a cap — already how he tells a commodity seller to
  escape the hourly comparison.
- **Form.** A price sheet per product with three named tiers whose differences are real deliverables; a rule set
  for the worker (§3.4): present the top tier, step down on "budget" by removing scope, never by lowering a
  price; a rush fee for a real rush; payment-plan terms the CEO has pre-approved.
- **First test.** The first ten proposals all lead with the top tier; record which tier is bought.
- **Needs the CEO.** The prices, the tiers, the plan terms, whether a rush fee exists.
- **Risk.** Leading high with nothing to justify it — each line of the top tier needs an evidence item.

### 8. The talk-to-the-founder price
- **What.** SF2 12:05: checkout price, and a higher price if the owner's personal time is wanted — "state the
  fact". The holding has exactly one human; his hour is its scarcest input.
- **Form.** Standard packages bought through the worker and a payment link; a call with the CEO offered as a
  paid option or reserved for orders above a size.
- **Needs the CEO.** Whether he wants this at all, and the threshold.
- **Risk.** Early on, a founder call may be what wins the first clients; this is a later lever, not a first one.

### 9. Partners one level up, paid in status
- **What.** SF1 22:16–27:16. The referrer wants to look good to their own client, not a fee; and one node (a
  brokerage) is worth twenty agents. "You want integration" — be the one name in their client packet.
- **Where.** B84 client-finding.
- **Form.** An offer to nodes that meet new businesses first — accountants, company-formation agents, business
  banks' advisers, commercial landlords, franchise head offices: "every client you send gets [a named upgrade]
  at the standard price", plus a page they can hand over.
- **First test.** Five such nodes in one city, each given the measured brief of their *own* site as the opener.
- **Needs the CEO.** The upgrade given away; any written partner agreement (a contract).
- **Risk.** A partner channel needs a relationship owner — he predicts that bottleneck himself.

### 10. Sell the next thing mid-flight
- **What.** SF2 10:05: ask at the half-way and two-thirds points, not after delivery.
- **Form.** In a site build, the monthly care / SEO package is offered when the first design is approved and
  again at pre-launch — with the launch measurements as the reason — not a month after hand-over.
- **First test.** The first client.

### 11. What he does to earn, that we can run
Visible in these four videos only (the other analysts own his funnel): full free training with a mid-video
invitation to a paid workshop; a recruiting call inside a sales lesson; an internal training chapter released as
a PDF lead magnet (`DISCO` is one); a free self-assessment that places a business on a stage map and prescribes
the next fix (shown on screen in both Scale or Fail episodes); a show where he diagnoses a business in public and
gives a verdict. Ours: release the office's own gates and checklists ungated; a **site-stage self-assessment**
that returns the next three fixes; a public teardown series — real sites measured, the fix shown, with the
owner's consent.

## 2. The larger picture — a first-place sales machine for a one-human holding

Picture the path of one enquiry. A dentist fills in the form at 21:40 on a Sunday. Within a minute a reply
arrives that says it is the studio's AI assistant, repeats what she asked for, and asks two questions. While she
answers, the brief worker crawls her site and one rival's and writes three findings. She is offered "now, here in
chat" or two real call slots; she picks Monday 08:15. At 08:15 the conversation opens with proof, promise and
plan in twenty seconds, then nine steps of discovery in which she talks two thirds of the time and hears her own
problem recapped in her words and labelled in ours — *found, chosen, answered, kept*. Authority, timing and
budget band are asked here, where they cost nothing. The pitch takes two minutes: three pillars, one picture
each, each tied to what her last agency lacked, then one honest paragraph about how long it takes and what nobody
controls. The top tier is named first, itemised, capped. If she hesitates the worker says her words back, asks
what the main concern is, answers it once from a library where every answer is true, and asks once. A yes stops
the selling: a payment link under terms the CEO approved once; anything outside those terms appears on his phone
as one approval. The delivery worker is introduced by role with her goal in its first sentence, the next meeting
is already dated, and onboarding starts within a day. A no gets a dated pulse-check and the brief to keep.

Every turn of that is logged with its stage. On Friday the tape review says where the week's losses sat and
proposes one change. Delivery reads the same tapes and strikes any sentence it cannot keep. The objection counts
update the FAQ on the site, which answers the next buyer before they ask.

Three things make this first-place rather than merely automated. **Speed and preparation at a level no human
team sustains** — his own multipliers, applied to every lead. **A measurement where others use persuasion** — the
buyer sees her own numbers, so the method needs none of the staged devices. **A script that is the only
variable** — so the machine learns from thirty conversations what a human floor learns from three hundred.
And the same machine, pointed outward, is a product: the front desk we sell to the very firms we measured.

The honest limits: nothing here has met a buyer; his figures are from consumer and small-business sales in the
US, mostly by phone; an AI seller's reception by buyers in each market is unmeasured; and by his own account
(UST 124:24) none of this fits committee purchases.

## 3. Skill candidates

Written to be authored from. Shared rules first; the skills refer to them by number.

### 3.1 The honesty rules (bind every sales skill; a message that breaks one is not sent)

| # | Rule |
|---|---|
| H1 | **Say who we are.** First message or first sentence: the studio and why it writes; everything said is true. Never posing as several people. That the seller is an AI is not announced (the CEO, 2026-11-23). |
| H2 | **No invented people or conversations.** A third-party example comes from the case library with a date and permission, or is not used. |
| H3 | **No invented numbers.** A figure about the prospect comes from a tool run kept on file; a figure about the market carries its source; a figure of ours is our own measured record. Otherwise: "I don't have a number for that." |
| H4 | **No invented scarcity or urgency.** Real calendar, real capacity, real deadlines only. A bonus or price change follows a written rule that applies to everyone. |
| H5 | **Answer the question.** Clarify once if it is ambiguous, then answer truthfully — including "no". The under-the-hood answer is allowed only when the answer truly depends on an inspection, and then the inspection is offered. |
| H6 | **One honest paragraph before price**: time to first result, what the client must do, what nobody controls (rankings, platform rules, auctions), what is and is not guaranteed. |
| H7 | **The ask budget.** Per concern: one clarification, one answer, one ask. A second no on the same concern → propose a dated next step and stop. "Not interested" / "stop" → stop at once and confirm no further contact. |
| H8 | **No pressure on character or relationships.** No "the reason you're stuck is that you can't decide", no "permission or support", no shaming about money. |
| H9 | **Compliments only when specifically true; never reused as an argument.** |
| H10 | **Turn away the wrong buyer.** If the measured brief or discovery shows the purchase will not serve them, say so and say what would. Log it as a win for the method. |
| H11 | **Never speak ill of a named competitor.** Hand the buyer criteria they can check themselves, and meet them. |
| H12 | **The price is the same for everyone.** Change scope or pre-approved terms; never the price to close. |
| H13 | **Nothing said in the sale may be missing from the delivery.** Every promise is written into the order summary the buyer and the delivery worker both receive. |
| H14 | **Reviews and referrals**: ask every client; no reward tied to a rating; no filtering by satisfaction. |
| H15 | **Never mention the holding's AI business or income to a seller or vendor** (existing holding rule). |

Conduct in every stage: questions outnumber statements; the prospect holds about two thirds of the words; one
question per message in chat; "wow, that's expensive" is a remark, not an objection — acknowledge and go on.
Channel notes: **chat** — stages 1–5 may run asynchronously over hours; **e-mail** — stage 1 and the brief in the
first mail, at most three questions per mail, stage 6–7 as a one-page proposal; **voice** — the tone guide
applies (slower and lower at the price and the ask), and disclosure in the first sentence.

### 3.4 Price rules for the worker

1. Name the top tier first, to everyone; describe it by its deliverables.
2. Itemise: each line is something the buyer can check after delivery.
3. State the cap ("it will not cost more than this unless the scope we wrote changes; if it does, you approve the
   change first").
4. On "budget": remove scope to reach the lower tier; say what is lost.
5. On "can you do it for less": H12 sentence; offer scope or pre-approved terms.
6. A rush fee only for a real rush, stated as a rule.
7. Monthly packages: renewal terms said at purchase; a reminder before each renewal; cancelling takes one message.
8. Anything outside the sheet — a custom price, a custom term, a contract clause — is not promised; it becomes
   one approval item for the CEO, and the buyer is told when to expect the answer.

### 3.6 The skills

**`sales-first-touch`**
- *Triggers:* any new enquiry on a form, chat, inbox or missed call; target first reply ≤ 60 s, any hour.
- *Inputs:* the enquiry text, source page or ad, client record if one exists.
- *Steps:* (1) H1 line; (2) restate what they asked, with the page or ad it came from; (3) at most three
  qualifying questions (need, timing, who decides) — skip any already answered; (4) offer "continue here now" or
  the two nearest real slots; (5) start `sales-prospect-brief`; (6) write the record; score the lead green /
  yellow / red on need, authority, timing.
- *Output:* a reply sent; a record with score, answers and the next dated step. Red leads get a useful pointer
  and no pursuit.

**`sales-prospect-brief`**
- *Triggers:* a qualified lead, an outreach target, or 24 h before any booked conversation.
- *Inputs:* the prospect's domain; one named rival (theirs if given, else the top local result for their main
  service); tool access.
- *Steps:* (1) crawl and measure — phone load time, indexable pages, title and description as shown, structured
  data, the contact path; (2) visibility — whether they appear for their own main service in their place, in
  search and in AI answers; (3) the same for the rival; (4) choose three findings, each *fact → what a customer
  feels → what it costs*; (5) attach each tool run; (6) refuse to output a finding without a run behind it (H3).
- *Output:* a one-page brief; three one-line findings for the reminder and the opener; a list of what was not
  measured.

**`sales-show-up`**
- *Triggers:* a conversation is booked.
- *Steps:* confirm at booking with the agenda; at 24 h — one real finding from the brief ("we found three things;
  the first is…"); the morning of — time and link; 1 h before — one line. If they do not appear: one message with
  two new real slots; then the no-for-now cadence.
- *Output:* reminders sent and logged; show / no-show recorded.

**`sales-conversation`**
- *Triggers:* the booked time, or "continue here now".
- *Inputs:* the brief, the record, the price sheet, the question bank, the objection library, the honesty rules.
- *Steps:* stages 1–11 of §3.2 in order; each turn tagged with its stage; no stage skipped (a buyer who opens
  with "just the price" gets the range first — L17 — and then the two questions the exact figure depends on).
- *Output:* one of — paid order; approval item for the CEO; dated next step; closed with reason (one of the five
  families, or "not a fit — turned away"). Always: the order summary or the brief sent to the buyer; the tape.

**`sales-objection`** (called by stage 8)
- *Inputs:* the buyer's last message, the library, the ask budget state.
- *Steps:* (1) decide remark or refusal; (2) acknowledge in their words; (3) if the concern is unnamed, one of the
  five questions of PLAYBOOK §5.2 — never "what are you afraid of" before the main concern has been asked;
  (4) match a library entry; no match → answer plainly and log a new entry; (5) one ask; (6) apply H7.
- *Output:* the reply; the entry used; the budget state.

**`sales-handshake`**
- *Triggers:* payment received or approval granted.
- *Steps:* order summary with every promise (H13) → introduce the delivery worker by role and by the buyer's goal
  → first working session dated within 24 h → the right to withdraw stated in plain words → notes to delivery →
  at the mid-point and two-thirds point of a build, the dated moment to offer the monthly package.
- *Output:* next meeting booked; delivery has the tape and the summary.

**`sales-follow-up`**
- *Triggers:* no reply, or "not now".
- *Steps:* one message at 2 days with something new and useful from the brief; one at 7 days asking only whether
  to keep the file open; then a pulse check at the date they named, else at 3–6 months, each carrying one fresh
  measurement. A light line is welcome — his meme needs no repair, humour is not a trick — but
  never guilt. Stop on request, at once.
- *Output:* dated log; re-engaged / closed.

**`sales-tape-review`**
- *Triggers:* weekly, or every 30 conversations.
- *Steps:* (1) count outcomes by stage and by objection family; (2) check ten random tapes against the honesty
  rules — any breach is reported first; (3) find the stage where losses concentrate; (4) propose one change with
  the tapes that motivate it; (5) update library counts; (6) list every promise made, for delivery to confirm.
- *Output:* a one-page review; one proposed change; updated library. A change to price or offer goes to the CEO.

**`sales-process-diagnosis`** (also a service the office can sell or give as a first conversation)
- *Triggers:* a client says "I need more leads", or our own numbers stall.
- *Steps (his order, back to front):* the offer — can a buyer tell it from the cheaper one, in what unit is it
  sold; the conversion — time to first reply, script, the share lost to price, the ranked questions; the buyer —
  who pays most per job; the traffic — last.
- *Output:* a one-page verdict with the first fix, in his table form (PLAYBOOK §8.2).
