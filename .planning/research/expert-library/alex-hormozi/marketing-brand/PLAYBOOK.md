## 1. The frame: only four ways to be known, and almost nobody does them

**The core four** [M85 37:26]. Two questions cross: one-to-one or one-to-many; people who know you or strangers.

| | People who know you (warm) | Strangers (cold) |
|---|---|---|
| One-to-one | Warm outreach | Cold outreach |
| One-to-many | Posting content | Running paid ads |

"If you're not spending your day doing one of those core four things, you are not advertising." At a gym
conference nobody raised a hand to "who spends the first four hours of the day promoting?" — "This is why you're
small" [M85 38:27]. Under roughly $1–3M a year, the first four hours of every day go to promotion.

**Rule of 100** [cap:2026-11-10; reel:DfieHm4ICHc]. 100 primary actions a day for 100 days on ONE channel:
100 warm reach-outs, or 100 cold reach-outs ("expect lower response rates, so use automation"), or 100 minutes
making content with at least one piece released daily, or 100 minutes making paid ads, run 100 days straight.
"That's 10,000 actions… you tend to get results." A larger business re-applies the rule to each NEW channel or
platform it opens.

**More, better, new** [M85 06:06–16:13].
1. *More* first. "Volume negates luck." His own shock moments: a mentor posting 5x/day on LinkedIn while he
   had not posted; "we post 50 plus times a day" versus a billionaire friend's once a day; a portfolio CEO
   making 15 ads a month when Hormozi made 35 a week at that size.
2. *Better* when one unit of improvement beats one more unit of volume (10 salespeople: an 11th adds 10%; a
   headline test may add 20%).
3. *New* last — "the cost of change is guaranteed, the return is not." Founders want 80% of time on new; he
   says 90% on more and better of what works. New only when the channel is truly saturated (a local gym
   reaching everyone in its radius several times a week).
4. Constraint question: "What would stop me from 10x-ing the business right now?" Find what breaks first, fix
   it, raise volume, repeat [M85 12:09].
5. A new channel takes 3, 6, sometimes 12 months. Judge it by progress, not dollars: clicks → opt-ins → right
   people → conversations → sales — "walking the dollar over the bridge" [M85 15:11].

**Create flow, monetize flow, then add friction** [M85 06:06]; "nail it, then scale it" [M85 40:27]. Run water
through the pipe before optimising it.

**The staging rule** [M85 40:27]: 0→six figures = one product, one avatar, one channel. Six→seven figures = the
same, made consistent. Seven→eight = add a second product (an upsell). Multiple eight = add a second channel.
The avatar stays ONE all the way.

**Advertise front to back, build the business back to front** [M85 72:43]. Effort in an ad goes to the first
seconds; effort in the business goes to what a customer is worth, because that sets what you may spend to win
one. "All advertising works. It's just a matter of efficiency" — replace "Facebook ads don't work for us" with
"I don't know how to make them work yet".

TEST. The four-box is a clean, complete taxonomy and I found nothing that falls outside it (referrals,
affiliates, agencies are other people doing the same four — his "lead getters", §9). "Volume negates luck" is
sound for learning speed; it is not sound as licence to publish thin material — he himself corrects it with
"qualume" (§7.2). The staging rule is an observed pattern, not a law (he names AG1 as an exception).

## 2. Getting the first customers

### 2.1 Start free, for a stated trade [M85 00:01–06:06; F5 03:01]
His three starts: personal training paid as a donation to the client's chosen charity (first ~13 clients);
gym turnarounds done free, paying his own flights and ad spend, paid only on sales made (30+ markets, later
licensed to "5,000+ locations"); free users for his software company to get case studies.

Free is a trade, said out loud: "I plan on going to this price, but I'll do it for free in exchange for"
(1) you actually use it, (2) feedback — "tell me how I could make it worth it for you to stay and pay",
(3) a public testimonial "if you think it deserves one", optionally (4) a referral — testimonial and referral
contingent on a good job. The real test is the fourth thing: do they pay full price afterwards.

Why: free customers convert to paying, refer, leave reviews, and give feedback; and "in the beginning, you suck"
— a free label buys grace while the product is fixed. It is not only for beginners: any new product or division.

**Why even free gets a no** [F5 06:03]: hidden costs. Ask the refuser why; remove the costs; later you can
charge more than rivals who keep them. His own example: a stranger wrote "I want to do short form videos for
you. You don't have to do anything. I will take all the stuff you already have online, make them and even post
them. You just need to say yes." He said yes, and later paid.

### 2.2 The price ratchet
- After the free batch ("10 or 20 people"), raise 20% every five customers "until the conversion rate times the
  price goes down" [M85 04:04].
- The simpler script version [F5 07:03]: free → 80% off for the next five → 60 → 40 → 20 → full → above full.
- When capacity fills you have true scarcity and can say so honestly; tell free clients a full-price client
  wants the slot and ask whether they will match; if not, part warmly and collect the review [F5 08:03].

### 2.3 The ten steps (warm outreach) [F5, whole]
1. **Build the list**: every email contact, every social follower you can DM, every phone contact.
2. **Pick one platform** — the one where you have most contacts.
3. **Personalise**: 30 seconds on their profile; open with something true about them ("saw you switched jobs").
4. **Reach out to 100 a day.**
5. **Warm up with A-C-A**: Acknowledge what they said, Compliment it, Ask a question that leads toward your
   field ("you must be a supermom — how do you have time to eat right and work out?").
6. **Ask who they know, not whether they will buy**: "By the way, do you know anybody who is [their struggle]
   looking to [dream outcome] in [time]? I'm taking on five case studies for free because that's all I can
   handle. I just want testimonials. I just had [name] work with me to get [outcome] even though [same
   struggle]. Does anyone you like come to mind?" — and if no: "Haha, does anyone you hate come to mind?"
   The short version: "I help [type of customer] get [dream outcome] in [time] without [effort and sacrifice]
   and I guarantee [X]."
7. **Make it free** on the three terms of §2.1. A *lead* is someone you can contact; an *engaged lead* has
   shown interest.
8. **Go back to the top of the list** — "if you reach out to 3,000 people with a free thing you'll get five".
9. **Start charging once referrals arrive** (the ratchet).
10. **Keep the list warm**: check in like a human, give value, post results, until they ask you.

TEST. The script is honest only when its facts are true (five slots really is the limit; the named previous
client exists). For a holding with no personal network the list in step 1 is nearly empty — the method survives
as *cold* outreach only if the "free thing" is made so concrete that a stranger sees it before deciding (idea 1
of FOR-THE-HOLDING). His own inbound example (the short-video stranger) is exactly that case.

### 2.4 +BOOK — what the book adds to cold outreach (unverified today)
Three problems and their fixes: you cannot reach them → build a targeted list (software scrape, brokers, by
hand); they do not know you → personalise and lead with "big fast value" (give something useful before
asking); response is low → volume: automate delivery, follow up more times and in more ways. Rule of 100
applies ✓.

## 3. Hooks, callouts and the 80/20 of an advertisement

**The claim** [M85 17:13–24:18]. Ogilvy: once the headline is written, 80 cents of the dollar are spent.
Hormozi: "I do 10 times the effort on the first 5 seconds… if I have an hour, 55 minutes on the first 5
seconds." The headline is also the *frame* through which the rest is consumed: two thumbnails on the same video
change average view duration "by two or three minutes"; a landing-page headline change alone raised booked
calls 62% with the opt-in rate unchanged [M85 21:14, a portfolio company, his claim].

Why the front: back-end steps improve by tens of percent (a show rate from 50% to 75% is huge); a
click-through rate can go from 1% to 4% — a 4x on everything downstream [M85 23:17].

Order of importance in an ad [cap:2026-10-04]: (1) get attention — visual or verbal hook; (2) tell them what
to do — the CTA; (3) why — everything else.

**Rules for the words** [M85 21:14–22:16]. Assume they do not know who you are, what you do, how it works,
are in a rush and read at third-grade level. "Clear beats clever, deletion beats explanation." Cut to the
shortest form, then run it through a reading-level checker until it reads at grade 3.

**The callout** [M85 24:18, reading p.136 of *$100M Leads*]. "Whatever you do to get the attention of your
audience." A dropped tray makes everyone look; "Mom!" makes mothers look; your name makes only you look. Make
it "as specific as possible to get the right people but broad enough to get as many of them as I can". Three
forms: a problem they have; a question they will answer yes to; a plain label ("Homeowners in Clark County").
The reader should think "this is for me". More specific usually yields MORE qualified leads, not fewer.
Where to find them: competitors' ads to the same audience — the ones that have run a long time are working.

**The weekly ad session** [M85 31:25–34:25].
1. *Always collecting*: he keeps no ad-free premium accounts "because I want to hear the ads"; screenshots and
   recordings go to one swipe folder.
2. Once a week, same day as recording, first thing in the morning ("cram for the test"): open the swipe file,
   then re-watch the first 10–30 seconds of his own best ads of the last 1–3 years.
3. Write **50 hooks: 40 are remakes of proven winners, 10 are new experiments.** Most people invert this.
   The 10 are tomorrow's control candidates.
4. Write **3–5 "meats"** — an educational point, a belief to break, a list, steps, a story, a proof.
5. Write **1–3 CTAs**.
6. Record. Hooks × meats × CTAs give the combinations: "50 times three times three… it's 450 ads"
   [reel:DfgNZ9VhWjc]. The results name the top five hooks, the best meat and the best CTA.
7. Next session: say the best hooks again with a different top, background or setting; keep the best meat
   and CTA; "then you create new hooks and try to beat the control."

**The kaleidoscope — squeeze a winner** [M85 34:25–37:26]. "That top 5% makes 95% of your income." When an ad
wins (his example: a customer in front of his full gym, wife beside him, telling six months of change), make
variants instead of looking for the next idea: (1) different background or colour; (2) props or visuals;
(3) re-enact it word for word; (4) re-order it; (5) visual filters; (6) visual effects; (7) fonts and
captions; (8) pacing/speed; (9) music; (10) keep the hook, change the body. "Squeeze the living hell out of
the winners."

**Awareness decides the ad** [M85 75:45–79:46]. Eugene Schwartz's five levels: unaware → problem aware →
solution aware → product aware → most aware (customers). Beginners profit only from the most aware (their list).
A spend ceiling ("can't get past $2,000 a day") usually means the ads are not good enough for the next, colder,
far larger ring. "The size of the plane is directly correlated with the length of the runway": the colder the
person and the higher the price, the more selling the ad and the path must do; a warm message to a cold
audience fails. Returns fall as you go colder (20:1 → 5:1 → 3:1) while absolute profit rises — "a watermelon
slice: fewer seeds per bite further down".

**+BOOK ad anatomy** (unverified today): callout → value → CTA. Value is built with "what–who–when": the
value-equation elements (outcome, likelihood, speed, ease and their opposites) told from different persons'
viewpoints (the buyer, spouse, children, rivals) and times (past, present, future). CTA: what to do, a reason
to do it now, plain words ✓. The landing page must repeat the ad's promise.

TEST. The 62% and 1%→4% figures are his, unverified, plausible in direction. "30% of people can barely read"
[M85 22:16] is loose: the OECD adult-skills survey (PIAAC 2023) puts US adults at or below literacy level 1 at
about 28% (from my memory of the survey, not re-checked today) — "barely read" overstates what level 1 means, but the instruction (write simply) stands and matters
more for a multilingual audience. The Larry King story (re-shooting 30 seconds turned $250k of dead spend into
$100M of sales) is a second-hand anecdote. The 40/10 rule presupposes a history of winners; a new advertiser
has none and must borrow competitors' long-running ads as its "proven" set — he says exactly this about callouts.

## 4. Lead magnets and "give away the secrets, sell the implementation"

**Definition** [M85 65:40]. A lead magnet, like a piece of content, is "a complete solution to a very narrow
problem". The buyer uses the free result as a forecast of the paid one.

**Rules** [M85 65:40–72:43].
- "Make your free stuff better than their paid stuff": only give away what you would be willing to charge
  $100, $1,000, $10,000 for.
- "99% of people who consume your free thing will never buy from you — your reputation is made by the 99%."
- Give away the low-ticket product too; it earns little and its buyers are a burden. He has "gotten less and
  less into down-sells": 10% of calls buying a thing at one tenth of the price moves revenue 1%.
- Put CTAs inside the free thing and follow up hard.
- **The CTA is about personalisation, not about more**: not "if you want more like this, go here" but "if you
  want me to help apply this to your business, let me know".
- "Deep is where the dollars are": a registered-dietitian-billing account with 5,800 followers earning "just
  under a million dollars a year in profit", 10–30 likes a post, selling 5–10 cheat sheets a day at ~$99 plus
  2–3 high-ticket members a week [M85 66:40; RICH 01:01]. A friend's dense SEO content gets little engagement
  and brings Fortune 500 executives saying "can we just hire you?" [M85 69:42].

**+BOOK the seven steps ✓**: (1) pick the problem and who has it; (2) decide how to solve it; (3) decide how
to deliver it; (4) test what to name it; (5) make it easy to consume; (6) make it good; (7) make it easy for
them to tell you they want more. **Three types ✓**: *reveal their problem* (a diagnosis; strongest when the
problem gets worse with waiting); *sample or trial* (brief full access to the core offer); *one step of a
multi-step process* (one valuable step free, the rest sold). **Four delivery forms ✓**: software (tools,
calculators, dashboards), information (courses, guides, events), services (free work — audits, ad management),
physical products. Naming is tested headline first, then image, then subheadline ✓.

**His own lead magnets, as observed** (the method applied):
- *Personalised Scaling Roadmap* — "just enter your info and it'll spit it right back to you… what the
  constraint feels like, the symptoms, what steps we took to graduate", by headcount stage and eight business
  functions, plus "a 90-minute video that tells you what to do" [PB27 06:03; SOF 25:14]. Type: reveal their
  problem. Delivery: software + information. The same 40-second read-out is inserted into at least three of
  the seven videos [PB27 06:03; ALGO 15:07; RICH 05:01].
- *Free video courses of both books* at acquisition.com/training and as podcast audio [BRAND 35:29].
- *$100M Sales Teams* — a free four-hour training, the "one big thing" of 2026 [reel:DfzyEY9Iu_o].

**The landing page for a lead magnet** [reel:Df3SUd3oVhP, complete].
1. Headline: stop them and promise one clear outcome.
2. Subheadline: adds context to that promise (the title-and-thumbnail relationship).
3. Hero image: proof of the headline — the thing they get or the outcome, shown.
4. CTA button: what they get and how they get it, nothing else.
5. Form: as few fields as still yield a *qualified* lead — "that could still be four or five"; he asks for more
   now than he used to ("I used to want more opt-ins, now I want more customers"). More than five fields →
   split into steps.
6. Optional one-line description answering "why should I give my email".
7. Below the fold: three bullets = the three biggest objections, most common first.
8. Social proof only if the thing is complex.
9. Everything else blank; longer pages usually convert lower; mobile first; compressed images; legal text.
10. The razor: "if it doesn't increase opt-in percentage of qualified leads, cut."

**The video sales letter for a lead magnet or offer** [reel:DgAgJx4Bt4u]: promise → pain → proof → plan →
their way versus our way → answer worries before they ask → one small question to confirm they watched → end
with many happy customers ("only the first 7–10 minutes you have to watch, the rest is just happy stories" —
those who reach minute 10 stay).

TEST. "Free better than paid" is how he actually operates (three free courses, a free four-hour training),
and it is the only method here that fits a firm with no proof. Its cost is real: the free thing must be
excellent, and the 99% statistic is his assertion, not a measurement. The dietitian figures are a single
second-hand case with no source; treat as illustration.

## 5. Proof: "state the facts and tell the truth", "say what only you can say"

**Track, then tell** [M85 42:27–47:32]. "If you don't track, you don't care." Measurement itself improves
results. Steps: collect data on every customer at day 1, 30, 60, 90 and at the end; improve until the data is
compelling; then publish it in one sentence with four variables:

> **[percentage or average of people]** achieve **[outcome]** in **[time or attempts]** under **[conditions]**.

Example: "the average gym makes over $100,000 a year in added profit after 12 months". He also publishes the
bottom: the bottom 20% averaged $40,000 added revenue — "basically broke even". **Fewer conditions beat bigger
numbers**: he prefers a lower result with fewer conditions to a higher one hedged with many.

**Clickbait, backed** [M85 42:27]. A top creator told him: "You absolutely make clickbait titles. You just
back it up with truth."

**The truth, whole** [ALGO 12:07–15:07]. "Imposter syndrome is you feeling ashamed for lying." His example: an
appointment-setter for agencies who pretended he had run a good agency; the true story (bad at delivery, good
at getting clients, so he does only that) sells better. "The truth sells the most… But it has to be the whole
truth. You have to say all the [parts] where you look bad too. And then people will believe the [parts] where
you look good." Stay inside your authority: asked to talk about happiness, he declines.

**Say what only you can say, show what only you can show** [M85 47:32–52:35]. "How I" beats "how to". Two
steps: "do epic stuff, talk about what you did". Two ways to earn the right: *outcome* (sold a million
copies) or *out-work* (wrote two books; went on 100 dates and cut it to 16 minutes). **Value per second**,
not seconds of value: compress long work into a short piece — "that's why summaries always work". And "if you
want to sell to rich people, promise the same result in half the time for twice the price".

**Demonstrate instead of claim** [M85 49:34–52:35]. An agency plays a one-minute recording of a real lead it
generated: "how many of these could you take a month?" A software seller edits the prospect's own video live
on the call. A door-to-door seller removes a real stain from his gym turf and sells $100 of cleaner. The
buyer discounts every promise by how unlikely it feels for *him*; a demonstration on his own material removes
the discount.

**The trust economy** [ALGO 10:07–12:07]. "We're in the show-me times, not the tell-me times." Demonstrate
expertise "in as many varied conditions as possible", with the avatars you serve, solving their problems your
way. "Education is the door": instructions that are easy to follow (words they understand) and worth
following (conviction from proof). "The proof is not in the pudding, it is the pudding."

**+AIC — proof against AI content.** Creators sit on a risk continuum: entertainer → B2C educator → prosumer
(money) → B2B. The higher the stakes of following the advice, the more third-party proof the audience demands,
and the later AI avatars displace the human [+AIC 00:00–06:01]. So: "How can I do demonstration at scale?"
(1) Transcribe every meeting and ask AI for the interesting moments — real examples, not invented ones.
(2) Build proof into delivery — "a self-licking ice cream cone": customers buy, the buying generates
marketing, marketing brings customers. (3) "If you only have 10 customers, start doing audits for people for
free and document them… who says no to that?" (4) "I'm usually not making content. We're just capturing it"
— document while you deliver [+AIC 08:01–12:02].

TEST. This is the strongest and most durable part of his teaching, and it agrees with the search-side
experts in this library (evidence a model cannot invent). One caution on his own practice: his Skool figure is
quoted as 30.44% in one place and 33.44% in another ("of people who finish their first month make their first
dollar" [M85 45:29; +7.8M 42:16]; a 2024 video title says 54.1%). "Who finish their first month" is exactly
the kind of condition he tells others to strip, and it is a survivor filter. Use the four-variable sentence;
state the denominator.

## 6. Brand

**Definition** [BRAND 05:04]. He rejects the usual definitions ("gut feeling", "what they say it is")
because none tells you what to do. His: "Branding is a deliberate pairing of things through an outcome."
Coca-Cola (thing) + drinking (action) + yum (outcome). *Advertising* is "letting people know about your
stuff"; *branding* is the pairing that results. Branding always happens; *good* branding is deliberate
pairing with things your ideal customer likes.

**The case** [BRAND 06:04–10:06; PB27 05:02]. The 2023 Bud Light influencer campaign was "good advertising,
bad branding": many people learned of it, most of that brand's core buyers disliked the pairing, sales fell;
the brand then paired with UFC and a comedian its buyers like and recovered. His point is audience-relative:
"there's no inherently good or bad association" — the same pairing could suit another brand. Second case
[PB27 07:04]: a Coca-Cola AI-made Christmas ad drew backlash because, for a mass audience, "AI itself has a
brand" (job loss, fear); "Couldn't Nvidia have an AI ad? Absolutely."

**How a brand makes money** [BRAND 13:11–19:17; cap:2026-10-24]. A weak mark paired repeatedly with what the
buyer admires becomes strong; a strong mark turns a commodity into a premium product (the $5 shirt); buyers
pay to associate themselves with the outcome. Results: pricing power, higher response to ads (his
illustration: 0.5% → 3% click-through), loyalty, protection from rivals. Caption form: "a strong brand allows
you to convert a higher percentage of customers at higher prices and pay better people less money — higher
ROAS, higher LTV, higher gross margins, higher net profit."

**Building it** [BRAND 19:17–27:21; PB27 03:02].
- Choose the pairings ("flowers on a table"); repeated pairing makes the bouquet. Distant, random pairings
  weaken. Narrow = repeat one topic; broaden = step to adjacent topics.
- Choose equally what to avoid, remove, ignore.
- Visual consistency is a pairing: beard, cap, tank top, flannel "every single day" until people wear it as a
  costume; black turtleneck = Jobs.
- A bad pairing is not erased; it is drowned by many good ones.
- **After purchase the product does the branding.** A hole in the shirt reverses everything back up the chain.
  If the product is merely "good enough", brand carries it; at a premium price the product must be dialled.
- The virtuous cycle [PB27 09:04]: what you say → what others say → what the buyer finds for himself — the
  film trailer, the friend's opinion, then your own verdict, which is the only one you keep. "Then basically
  wait 5 years and don't give up."
- Every new pairing risks part of the audience ("they sold out"); the bet is that you gain more than you lose.
  "Don't let the five mean comments stop you from gaining the 500 new people" [BRAND 31:25].
- Controversy is not required: a strong brand can be broadly positive [BRAND 28:22].

**Three metrics** [BRAND 27:21]: *influence* (does seeing it change behaviour), *direction* (toward or away),
*reach* (for how many). Practical test: when you say "click here, download this, attend this", how many do.

**Personal brand in three stages** [PB27].
1. **Do epic** — skills, results or volume of work. "You'll build a bigger brand making content for 6 months
   after a decade of experience than a decade of content with 6 months of experience." No result yet → show
   the work: "do a thousand outreach attempts… document it."
2. **Associate** yourself, repeatedly, with what your avatar likes — content about it, products free and
   paid, appearing beside people they like. Avatar: a market that is growing, in pain, can pay; ("easy to
   find" matters less now — "AI knows who it's for and will serve it directly" [PB27 04:02]).
3. **Defend the belt** — recurring, public, dated, high-stakes moments: Red Bull's space jump, Apple keynotes,
   his own company sale and book launches. Stakes make outsiders watch and align the team inside. "You're only
   as good as your last season."

TEST. The definition is classical conditioning stated plainly, and it is operational — you can list pairings
and audit them. What it leaves out: distinctiveness and memory structures (the Ehrenberg-Bass school would say
being easily recalled matters more than being liked), and it cannot be measured as crisply as he implies; his
"three metrics" are concepts, not instruments. The Bud Light sales fall is well documented; the size and
cause of the recovery is his reading. The Guinness-record book launch ("over $106M in 72 hours" [PB27 00:01],

## 7. Content: what to make, how to open it, how to manufacture it

### 7.1 What to make — buyers, not views
- **Entertainment is consumed; education changes behaviour** [+7.8M 02:00]. "Learning = same condition, new
  behaviour" [BRAND 04:03; cap:2026-11-02]. Entertainment audiences want more entertainment; they "do not
  become" education buyers "in any appreciable rate" [+7.8M 06:04].
- **The algorithm gives the wrong signal** [RICH 03:01–07:02]. In one quarter his six most-viewed videos
  (350k–1.2M views, beginner topics) "made no sales. Zero." The top revenue video had 270k views (how to
  segment customers); next were deep sessions with multi-million-dollar businesses at 100k–250k views. Two
  quarter-long experiments with broader content: views and subscribers up, book sales, leads and portfolio
  applications down [RICH 02:01]; in the earlier one views rose 2–3x while ad revenue halved [+7.8M 28:11].
- **Make videos for your buyers**: take the top 20% of customers by spend, find what they share and what
  problems they had, make content on those. "Be prepared to see your view counts go down… but your sales go
  up" [RICH 09:03]. The top-20% analysis is the Vista method [M85 56:36]: psychographics, geography,
  demographics, actions; cut to the two or three variables that cluster the best customers; sell only to those.
- **Vertical value** [RICH 07:02]: when going broad, pick topics where the beginner and the $100M owner both
  gain (e.g. "how the 1% think about money") — these get views and revenue.
- **Narrow** [+7.8M 22:08]: he dropped relationships, college, food, lifestyle for business only.
- **Metric** [+7.8M 24:10]: he moved from views to ad revenue (views × revenue per thousand) because it pairs
  quantity with audience quality and arrives daily; his best ad-revenue month was also the best month for book
  sales, opt-ins and applications. Tracking of sales per video: UTM links in the description plus a spoken CTA
  to a lead magnet [RICH 10:04]. General rule: pair every volume metric with a quality metric.
- **Shorts and longs are different audiences** [+7.8M 30:14]: "shorts viewers watch more shorts, long viewers
  watch more longs, customers buy more." Longs drive far more conversions. Shorts still earn their place when
  they send a person to long-form on another platform.

### 7.2 "Qualume" and the five-step algorithm [ALGO 00:01–10:07]
Content creation "is a manufacturing process". Volume and quality are a false choice; the target is
*qualume*. He applies the five steps of the Tesla manufacturing method (he cites a book by Tesla's former
president titled *The Algorithm*), in strict order:
1. **Question every requirement** — his three questions: "What does that mean? How do you know that? So
   what?" First signal of waste: time-on-task is 10 hours but delivery takes a week.
2. **Delete** — "delete so much that we have to add stuff back". Colour grading went: best videos had none.
   He shot one on an iPhone in a plane seat; it became a top-decile video. "The content is the container. The
   stuff inside is what matters."
3. **Simplify/optimise** — the most for the least: if grading is 40% of the hours and 5% of the quality,
   make 1.5–2 videos at 95% instead.
4. **Accelerate** — end every meeting with "who, what, when"; then ask time-on-task and "what are you doing in
   the next four hours that is more important?" Daily instead of weekly check-ins = "7x the speed of a team".
5. **Automate — last.** "The only thing more efficient than automation is elimination." AI makes people do
   step 5 first. Litmus: with AI, are views, leads, money up? "If your token costs go up month over month but
   your money isn't, you're blowing money on [things] that don't matter."

### 7.3 Packaging and the opening [+7.8M 10:05–22:08]
- **Vague → clear** thumbnails and titles. The "curiosity gap" doctrine comes from entertainers; for education
  "if they want that thing they will click on that thing, and then you just give them that thing."
- **Proof – Promise – Plan** intro (found by re-watching his top 35 educational videos): prove you know (why
  listen to you), promise what they will get, give the plan for getting there. Lead with whichever matters most
  for the piece; for a business educator, proof. Entertainment "confirms the thumbnail"; education proves
  competence. (Confirming still matters: a top creator stopped a rival's video at 5 seconds — "you didn't
  confirm the thumb" [M85 20:14].)
- **Meat = lists, steps, stories**, not vlog.
- **Language over "razzle"**: in 30 days of reels, 9 of the best 12 emphasised the message, 8 of the worst 12
  emphasised effects. One of his best long videos is 70–80% words on screen.
- **Effective production**: visualise data (charts for scale and change) instead of effects.
- **Pre-search over post-production**: "an ounce of pre-work is worth a pound of post" — a quarter of the
  former editing time, spent before recording, removed "90, maybe 95%" of post work.
- **Assume nothing**: title as if they do not know you ("Day in the life of a $200M/yr CEO", not "…of Alex
  Hormozi"); introduce yourself and why to listen every time; explain every reference; no inside jokes. Keep a
  fixed intro with small variations (the Simpsons' chalkboard) — the intro itself becomes a brand pairing.
- **+BOOK** (unverified today): a content unit = hook → retain → reward. Hook topics from five sources (far
  past, recent past, present, trending, manufactured); retain with lists, steps, stories; reward = value per
  second. "Give until they ask" — offers are either integrated into the piece or made intermittently.
  **+WEB** (Copyblogger's write-up of his 2022 video, youtube id 91D5hjMEADg): eight recorded CTA types
  inserted in the edit; long-form gets three CTAs — start, middle, end.

### 7.4 "We need to be reminded more than we need to be taught" [M85 79:46–84:51; +7.8M 36:15]
- "The news never changes, just the names." Teach the same idea through new stories and new avatars (mother
  and daughter, veteran, shop owner), new formats and new contexts (the value equation as book, audio, course,
  whiteboard; applied to services, software, products).
- Talk about *your* news — what you did this week — instead of chasing trends.
- You tire of your message long before the audience has noticed it (the Henry Ford "we haven't started
  running it yet" story). "We put out 50 plus pieces a day. I'm grateful if somebody watches one a week."
- **Repost your best piece of last year**; new followers never saw it. Reintroduce the greatest hits.
- The endless content wheel [M85 64:40]: comments and questions on content become the next content; so do
  the friction points customers hit inside your service.

### 7.5 Give away value, measured
"Make content only for them that's useful" [M85 68:42]. Value = the value equation applied to a piece: help
them toward a wanted outcome or away from a feared one — more certain, faster, easier [M85 61:39–64:40].

TEST. §7.1 is the most valuable correction in the slice and it rests on his own back-end data, which nobody
outside can check; the logic (the buyer population is small and rich) is sound regardless. The RPM proxy works
only on a monetised channel whose buyers are advertisers' favourites — a new channel has no such signal and
must use UTM-tracked opt-ins and replies instead. "Language over razzle" came from 24 reels of one creator
with a famous face; it is a hypothesis for a faceless AI-made brand, not a finding. The 7.8M talk is from
2024; platform behaviour has moved, but he repeats the same conclusions in the 2026–27 videos.

## 9. Referrals, events and other people's reach

**The referral engine** [SOF 03:04–10:08, 19:11].
- A cash credit ($500 to both) is weak for well-off clients. Make the referral "social capital positive":
  the friend receives a real treatment or product worth $1,000–2,000 at retail, low cost to you — and only
  through a client, never advertised.
- Build it into the membership: "you get five of our one-off treatments to give to friends".
- **Do not ask for referrals; ask "who do you want to bring?"** at fixed moments: at the close, at each
  booking, at the 90-day review when results are felt. BAMFAM — book a meeting from a meeting; every meeting
  sets the next and names the guest. The same person cannot be brought twice.
- Find the "wow moment" in the customer journey and place the ask there.
- Newsletter: add a P.S. — a quarterly draw for a year of the top service among friends and family the reader
  enters; every entrant gets a smaller gift. The draw lets you state the top price without pressure.
- Sales-side wording [reel:Df7k2zOhoID]: "If I could only talk to people like you all day my life would be
  awesome. Who else is as successful as you?" — and referrals "can make up 20 to 30% of sales and most reps
  don't ask" [reel:Dfsi_bnhENS].

**Event-based marketing for a high-trust, high-ticket local sale** [SOF 13:09–22:13]. Take the free
diagnostic (a body scan) to an event your buyers already attend; run a draw for a year of service to gather
everyone; while the 20-minute scan runs the person watches a video sales letter; close right after (if that
feels too fast, book the next meeting). "Can we do one a week?" Events that work enter a permanent yearly
calendar. Scalable version: ads → a paid half-day workshop ($99 "to get a little bit of riff-raff out" and a
card on file) → video → in-person close. His arithmetic: 300 attendees → 100 scans → 25 sales. (The owner's
follow-up: his town is seasonal; he built his own lecture series instead [SOF 30:19].)

**Bad agencies are a cost of doing business** [SOF 01:03]: "$55,000 on the bad agency… I just think it's cost
of doing business. It doesn't mean agencies suck. You have margin so that you can try a couple until one
works." **+BOOK ✓** on using agencies: hire one to learn a platform with a stated end date, pay extra to have
decisions explained, train your own team, then drop to consulting.

**+BOOK lead getters** (unverified today): customers who refer, employees, agencies, affiliates — each is
someone else running the core four for you; referrals come from goodwill (results faster, expectations beaten)
before they come from asking.

TEST. The "who do you want to bring" reframe is good design: it turns an ask into a benefit of membership.
Paid-entry draws are games of chance — see the boundary question in OPEN-QUESTIONS. The 300→100→25 arithmetic
is hypothetical, never observed in the episode.

## 10. The economics a marketer must know (owned by the offers analyst; kept short)

- **LTV:CAC is the one number** [M85 25:19–31:25]. LTV = lifetime *gross profit*, not revenue; CAC = all-in
  cost to get one paying customer. At least 3:1. Quick estimate: last year's gross profit ÷ number of customers
  (under-estimates, which is the safe side). His additional rule: recover CAC within the first 30 days.
- Paid ads are "an auction for attention": whoever earns most per customer can bid most — "an ethical
  monopoly".
- Lead-cost benchmarks are meaningless without conversion and gross profit (the bow maker: $20 lead, 1 in 5
  buys, $500 gross profit = 5:1).
- A huge early ratio signals room to scale; ratios compress with spend; judge absolute profit.
- **Expanding the market** [M85 52:35–61:39]: up-market, down-market, adjacent, narrower, broader — and most
  should go *narrower*. "You get far more leverage doing the same thing 100 times than 100 things once."
  "Better creates bigger. Bigger creates bloat." Generalist agency → the 8% of clients (IT firms) where it
  excels; another → family-law attorneys only.

## 11. One-page checklist a worker can run

1. One avatar, one product, one channel. Which box of the core four is today's 100 actions?
2. Is the first offer free for a stated trade (use, feedback, review, referral)? Is the price ratchet written?
3. Hooks: 50 written, 40 from proven winners (ours or long-running competitor ads), 10 new? Callout specific?
   Reading level grade 3? First five seconds got most of the hour?
4. A winner exists → ten kaleidoscope variants before any new concept.
5. Lead magnet: one narrow problem, solved completely; would we charge for it; CTA offers personalisation.
6. Landing page passes the ten-point reel list; nothing on it that does not raise qualified opt-ins.
7. Every claim is a four-variable sentence from tracked data, bottom quintile included; the whole truth.
8. Each piece opens with proof–promise–plan, assumes a stranger, is lists/steps/stories, ends in one CTA.
9. Content topics come from the top 20% of buyers' problems; success is tracked revenue or opt-ins, not views.
10. Before automating a step: questioned, deleted, simplified, accelerated?
11. Pairings: is this appearance, collaborator, format, tool something our buyer likes? Product dialled?
12. Referral ask scheduled at the wow moment; next meeting booked from this one.
