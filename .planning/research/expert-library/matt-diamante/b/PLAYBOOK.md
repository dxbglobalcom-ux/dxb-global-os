## 1. The whole method in one page

His agency does the same small loop for every client, and says so in almost every video:

1. **Foundation once:** Search Console verified, sitemap submitted, site indexed, speed and mobile
   fixed, Google Business Profile complete `[7R 6]` `[SM]` `[90 2]`.
2. **Every existing page gets one keyword** — an easy variation, not the head term — placed in title,
   meta description, H1, at least one H2 and the body. One page a day until the site is done `[552 1]`.
3. **Two new posts a week**, each answering a real customer question, chosen easy-first from a keyword
   gap against a competitor `[552 2]` `[10K 0]` `[MS 27]`.
4. **Four backlinks a month** `[COST 3]` `[END 3]`.
5. **Every new post gets three to five internal links** from pages that already mention the topic `[IL 4]`.
6. **A live monthly report** that tracks each month's posts as a cohort; whatever falls for a month or two
   is re-optimised `[LS 14]`.
7. **A weekly team session, "Why Doesn't This Rank?"**, on one page that is stuck `[WDR 0]`.
8. **Pages sitting at positions 11–20** are pushed onto page one with a nine-step re-optimisation `[P2]`.

His stated economics: $3,000 a month `[COST 5]` `[STORY 6]`; two posts a week is "our minimum
package" `[MS 27]`; clients "who pay us $40,000 a year" `[P2 0]`; results in 3–6–12 months `[10K 0]`
`[7R 8]`. His one-line philosophy, repeated: "It's all just SEO. If you create truly helpful content,
you'll likely get traffic from all these platforms" `[JUN 1]`.

## 2. Choosing the keyword — "easy first"

### 2.1 The principle (the "avalanche")

- Do not target the head term your competitor wants ("electrician New York City"); "let them fight over
  that keyword" `[EASY 0]`.
- Chain: head term → **Questions** tab → pick a question (seed) → **Keyword variations** → pick the one
  with low volume **and** low difficulty. Example: bike repair → how to repair a bike tire → *how to
  repair a tubeless mountain bike tire* (20 searches a month) `[EASY 1]`.
- Why 20 searches matter: one post a week at 20 searches each is ~1,000 visits a month after a year;
  two a week ~2,000 — and it is not linear: traffic "unlocks" harder keywords, "the avalanche SEO
  technique" `[EASY 3]`. Traffic is one of the three parts of Semrush's authority score, so easy traffic
  raises authority `[SEM 23]`.
- **No-volume SEO:** keywords showing zero or "n/a" volume with low or "n/a" difficulty are worth
  targeting because nobody else is; tools only estimate, and ~15% of daily Google searches are new
  `[CKR 3]` `[NB2 9]` `[SEM 25]`. A sub-question with 10 searches can be one H2 of a post ("that one
  paragraph … could potentially get 20 people to your site per month") `[SEM 25]`.
- Intent beats volume: "tankless hot water heater installation Boston" — volume n/a, KD 4, but the
  searcher already bought the heater: "that is money waiting to be made" `[NB2 9]`.
- Local: add the suburb or modifier. "plumber Hamilton" KD 39 vs "emergency plumbing Hamilton" KD 0
  `[90 1]`; "Ancaster plumber" KD 7, 70 searches `[ADS 5]`.
- **Never write "near me" on a page** — "a rookie mistake"; write the city `[SEM 20]` `[PA 4]`.
- One page, one main keyword; many keywords on one page is "the first mistake" `[CKR 2]`. Two of your
  own pages ranking for one keyword can be cannibalisation (clicks split) `[GPT 4]`.
- Prefer **evergreen** topics: his own traffic halved in the tool's estimate when one or two timely posts
  faded `[SEM 6]`.

### 2.2 The numbers he uses

| Rule | Value | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Keyword difficulty filter (Semrush KD) | 0–30 (he also types 29) | `[SEM 19]` `[GPT 1]` `[552 2]` `[BI 4]` |
| Variations kept per page | 5–10, "maybe seven" | `[SEM 26]` |
| KD he refuses for a new site | 38 ("not typically a keyword we would try … for a new client") | `[TOOLS 1]` |
| SEO-friendly local market | KD under 30 and cost per click over $10 | `[ADS 5]` |
| Publishing cadence | 2 posts a week minimum; 1–2 during a core update | `[10K 0]` `[JUN 1]` |

**TEST.** The thresholds are Semrush's scale. His own comparison shows the three tools disagree on the
same keyword: volume 720 / 450 / 3,600 and difficulty 38 / 28 / "easy" (Semrush / Ahrefs / Ubersuggest)
`[TOOLS 1-2]`. So "KD ≤ 30" cannot be carried to DataForSEO unchanged; the threshold must be
recalibrated on our tool (OPEN-QUESTIONS 2). His habit when tools disagree is sound: assume the harder
number and promise the lower volume `[TOOLS 5-6]`.

### 2.3 Seven ways to find what to write

1. **Questions customers ask you** — the first and best source `[BI 1]`; Sheridan: "the greatest keyword
   tool … is your own ears" `[MS 24]`.
2. **AnswerThePublic** with a broad word ("braces": 100+ questions) `[BI 2]`.
3. **Google "People also ask"** — expand a question to load more `[BI 3]` `[10K 1]`.
4. **Keyword gap** against a competitor, KD 0–30, sorted by volume — his preferred way. If a local
   competitor gives too little, swap in "a massive website in your industry" (he uses Neil Patel) `[BI 4]`
   `[SEM 21]`. To find rivals when you rank for little: open one competitor, then take *its* competitors
   `[SEM 16]`.
5. **ChatGPT**: "I need blog ideas for [topic]. Can you make some recommendations?" `[BI 4]`. Given a URL
   it returns sensible keyword groups (core service, local, action) but no data; the best keyword in his
   test was not on its list. Verdict: "a great starting point", then run through a real tool `[CKR 1-4]`.
6. **Search Console queries containing why / what / how / can** — a tab of his report `[LS 6]`.
7. **Semrush Topic Research** (content marketing → topic → question cards) `[10K 1]`.

Plus two from Sheridan `[MS 13, 25]`: Google autocomplete with "vs", "for", etc.; and an AI prompt on the
Big 5 (section 9).

### 2.4 Free keyword research inside Search Console `[GSC]`

1. Performance → Search results; tick clicks, impressions, average position; last 3 months (28 days is
   too fresh).
2. Sort queries by impressions. Pick one with many impressions, few clicks, position outside the top 3.
3. Click the query → Pages tab → click the page that owns it → **remove the query filter** → Queries now
   lists every variation that page ranks for (his example: 170 variations; set rows to 250).
4. For each high-impression variation: Ctrl-F it on the page. If it is absent or only in body text, add it
   — ideally rewrite an H2 around it or add a section. "We are showing up for this keyword although
   it's not even on this page."

### 2.5 The "ranking road map" for one keyword `[CKR 4-6]` `[WDR 1]`

Open the keyword's SERP analysis in the tool (impartial — not your browser's personalised results):

1. Referring domains of the top three **pages**. If a top result has 0–3, "we could potentially rank …
   without getting any backlinks".
2. Are the top results directories? Then you will not outrank them — **ask to be listed in them** (a lead
   source and a link "from the top ranking sites for that keyword").
3. Ctrl-F the keyword on each top page; check title, meta, headings (Detailed SEO extension); word count.
4. Decide: page length, where the keyword goes, whether links are needed.

## 3. Backlinks — when they are needed

He ran the same test twice on eight keywords `[NB1]` `[NB2]`: look at the page-one results' page authority,
referring domains and backlinks and ask whether the top result has the most.

- Look at **referring domains, not backlinks** (one site can link many times; he once had 392,000
  backlinks from one or two sites, removed them, rankings improved) `[SEM 3]`.
- Finding: the page with the most links is usually not first. A site with authority 11 and 224 referring
  domains outranked one with 51 and 7,000 `[NB1 3]`; a page with zero links ranked first for a KD-4 keyword
  `[NB2 9]`. Conclusion: "backlinks do play a big factor, but it's not the only factor" `[NB2 11]`; "I
  don't think they matter as much as people think" `[NB1 15]`.
- Reddit, Yelp, YouTube, Facebook rank without links on their own authority; skip them when benchmarking,
  but they can be outranked by better content `[GPT 4]`. When Reddit is first, get recommended in that
  thread `[NB1 4]`.
- Homepages collect links easily (bios, directories); inner pages do not `[NB1 12]`.
- The exercise he sets: for every target keyword, write down how many referring domains the top three
  pages have — that is your link target `[NB2 12]`. Semrush prints it: "you'll need 67 referring domains
  and optimised content" `[GPT 10]`.
- Authority score = link power + organic traffic + natural profile `[SEM 1]`. Links from sites in your
  niche set your "category" `[SEM 31]`. Most links are authority 0–10; 50–60 is a realistic ceiling;
  .gov/.edu nearly impossible; follow and nofollow mix is natural `[SEM 34-36]`. Red dots in the network
  graph = link networks; outgrow them with good links `[SEM 30]`.
- Where he gets links: competitor's referring domains → Active + Follow → open each → directories you
  can join free; Pinterest (verify the domain); content better than the linked piece, then ask the
  publisher to add or switch the link `[SEM 37-40]`; The Hoth, about $225 a link, four a month `[COST 3]`;
  a free outreach spreadsheet `[ADS 8]`; a YouTube video description linking to the page `[WDR 14]`.
  Never Fiverr `[NB2 12]`. A study he cites: position one has 3.8× the links of positions 2–10 `[7R 5]`
  (**ADDED:** that is Backlinko's study).
- Brand searches count: people googling your name is a legitimacy signal `[SEM 13]` `[NB1 15]`.

**TEST.** His eight-keyword test is honest and the conclusion holds for low-difficulty local and
long-tail keywords — which is exactly where he tells clients to play. It is not evidence for competitive
head terms (he says so: KD "pretty hard" needs 67 referring domains). Two of his link sources cross a
line: paying a site "20 or 50 bucks" to switch a link `[SEM 28]` and bought placements are link schemes
under Google's spam policies unless marked `rel="sponsored"`, and for the holding undisclosed paid
placement is hidden promotion. Sound version: directories and associations, suppliers and partners,
original data and tools worth citing, expert quotes, digital PR, and the "listed where the answer engines
read" work of section 8 — which is link building and AI visibility at once.

## 4. Writing the page

### 4.1 Placement — "the three kings"

Main keyword in **title tag, H1, first sentence/paragraph**; cluster keywords in H2–H4 and body;
variations are fine, exact match "can sound robotic" `[P2 3]`. Elsewhere he lists five places: title, meta
description, H1, at least one H2, content `[552 1]` `[GPT 21]`. Keyword as close to the start of title and
H1 as possible ("StealthGPT: everything you need to know", not the reverse) `[SEM 26]`. Keyword in the URL;
change a URL only on a new page with no backlinks, and 301 it `[P2 4]`. One H1 per page; H2s must support
the H1 `[CKR 6]` `[PA 4]`. Title up to 60 characters `[PA 6]`. Alt text on images `[P2 5]` `[7R 4]`.
Meta description does not rank but lifts click-through `[P2 4]`.

Human, not robot: "Furnace Repair in Hamilton — 24/7 emergency service", not "best Hamilton HVAC company
Hamilton Ontario furnace repair Hamilton" `[90 3-4]`. If you already rank first, change nothing `[90 5]`.

### 4.2 Match the winner, then slightly exceed

"Whoever these guys are, they're doing something that Google likes and we want to mimic that as much as
possible" `[WDR 5]`. For the top three results he compares:

| Dimension | His rule | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Keyword count | Count the exact keyword on each (Ctrl-F); use it as often or slightly more ("they use it four, five, four — I'd use it … maybe six") | `[GPT 22]` |
| Density | Match the #1's ("0.9% … try to match that or slightly more"); no fixed ratio — "different for every keyword" | `[WDR 6, 22]` |
| Local pages | Count service word and city word separately (competitor: "handyman" 21×, "Hamilton" 12×) and match | `[PA 7]` |
| Headings | Keyword in H1, in about four H2s "in some way", in at least one H3 | `[WDR 8]` |
| Word count | Roughly match; **count the real body text** — the extension said 5,500 words, the pasted body was 3,700; 4,100 was 3,100 | `[GPT 24-26]` |
| Content gaps | What is missing, outdated, badly formatted; "if they wrote 800 words, write 900; five tips, give six" | `[P2 5]` |
| Furniture | Table of contents, infographic, video inside the body, author page, external links to sources | `[WDR 5-16]` |

**TEST.** Google has said for years that neither keyword density nor word count is a ranking factor, and
his own material contradicts the counting ("only 5.4% of AI responses contain exact keyword matches";
"intent beats exact keywords" `[CHG 4]`). What the counting actually detects is real: a page that never
states the query's words in its headings, or covers half of what the winners cover. Sound version for
our workers: measure **coverage** — which sub-topics, questions and entities the top three cover that we
do not — and use counts only as a floor (is the phrase in title, H1, an H2, the opening?) and a ceiling
(are we above every winner — stuffing?). His warning on tools stands: no tool counts variants correctly
`[PA 7]`.

### 4.3 The content outline ("the cheat code") `[OUT]` `[10K]`

1. Blank doc; keyword at the top.
2. Google it; open the top three that are not giants (skip Amazon, Home Depot; skip Reddit, YouTube).
3. Detailed SEO extension → Headings → copy each page's headings into the doc.
4. Delete the irrelevant (menu, cart, "proudly brought to you by").
5. Write your own H1 with the keyword ("Home saunas: everything you need to know").
6. Turn the rest into H2/H3; add H2s from keyword research that **no competitor covered** ("basement
   sauna", "how much is a sauna in your house").
7. Add standard blocks: benefits, pros and cons, cost.
8. Ask the model to add H3s under each H2; then read every line and remove what is not true for you.

Question-led variant `[10K 1-4]`: question from the tool → collect People-also-ask questions → ask the
model for more related questions → those are the H2s → have the model summarise the top three results
("not to make it easier for us to read but so that we can train" it on the topic) → H3s → draft (his
example: 2,100 words) → fact-check, rewrite, add experience. "If you just post it like this … it's very
obviously written with AI."

### 4.4 Writing with AI without producing slop — the transcript-first method `[GPT]`

The core idea: the expertise goes in **before** the model writes, as a spoken transcript.

1. Keyword from the gap (KD 0–30) `[GPT 1]`.
2. **Film yourself answering the question** — phone front camera or Zoom — with your opinions, what you
   do for clients, what you tried, what failed. Upload to YouTube (unlisted is fine) and copy the automatic
   captions. Voice-to-text and talking to the model directly lost him a half-hour session `[GPT 7-9]`.
3. In one conversation: paste the transcript → "summarize this" (training, not reading) `[GPT 9]`.
4. Paste each of the top three ranking URLs → "summarize this" `[GPT 11]`.
5. Paste all their headings → "Can you clean up this list of heading tags and remove anything irrelevant?
   I'm writing a blog post called [title]" `[GPT 12]`.
6. **Edit the outline by hand against what you actually do.** He deletes "one-time projects and audits"
   and "pricing by business size" because his agency does neither; renames tiers to his real package
   names. "If I just had it write this … people come to my website and be mad" `[GPT 15-17]`.
7. "Ask me questions that you need to know in order to write a detailed blog post." It asks per section
   (who is the audience, what is in the $1,500 vs $3,000 package, have you had to justify the price …).
   Answer them — or let it answer from prior conversations, then correct `[GPT 19-20]`.
8. "Using the following outline and article summaries above as well as the initial transcript … write a
   fully optimized blog post. Include the main keyword in the title tag, meta description, H1, at least one
   H2 and several times within the content. You can use variations" `[GPT 21]`.
9. Human pass: fix facts, add a paragraph between an H2 and its first H3, put the **case study** in the
   "is it worth it" section, name your own tools and link them, state your margin if you wish `[GPT 16,
   26-28]`. "I call it a first draft because you're going to have to go in and fix it."
10. **Key takeaways block at the top** — for skimmers and so answer engines "can pull stats" `[GPT 14]`
    `[JUN 1]`. Answer the question within the first 150 words `[CHG 1]`.

Why it is not penalised, in his words: Google's helpful-content update (August 2022) demotes content
"created solely for ranking purposes"; what it wants is "brand new content from industry experts" — the
local pool guy's tips count `[BI 0]`. E-E-A-T made concrete `[CHG 2]`: owner's photo and credentials on
every service page; "In my 15 years as a plumber, I've seen …" instead of "plumbing is important";
testimonials with photos; author page linking to social profiles and notable guest posts `[WDR 15-16]`.
Output he claims with this method: one team member writes three to four posts **a day** `[COST 5]`.

**TEST.** This is the strongest thing in the set and it is current: Google's guidance on AI content is
about usefulness and first-hand value, not about how the text was produced. The method's weak point is
that it needs a human who talks. For an AI-run office that is the design question (FOR-THE-HOLDING 1).
Corrections: E-E-A-T is a quality-rater concept, not a set of "80 signals"; the first E is Experience.

## 5. Internal links, sitemap, indexing

**Internal links `[IL]`.** Crawl depth: more than three clicks from the homepage tells Google you do not
care about the page. Three kinds: navigation (the strongest signal of which pages matter: work, services
or products, contact, blog), in-content, footer (main services or top 5–10 product categories plus 5–10
blog categories — never more than 5–10 categories). Every page links to 3–5 others; one internal link per
200–300 words; anchor = the keyword of the target, never "click here". **After publishing:** search
`site:yourdomain.com keyword`, open each result, Ctrl-F the keyword, link it to the new post — an indexed
old page pulls the new one into the index. In Yoast's two link columns, any post with 0–2 incoming links
is raised to 3–5. Use one anchor for one target: the same phrase pointing at two of your pages and an
outside page "is confusing Google" `[WDR 11-13]`. Internal anchors can make a page rank for a variation
it never uses `[GPT 23]`. Blog index: 25–50 posts per page, not six — old posts must not be buried
`[WDR 21]`.

**Sitemap and indexing `[SM]` `[7R 0-2]` `[P2 6]`.** `site:domain.com` to see what is indexed; check
`/sitemap.xml` (Yoast: `/sitemap_index.xml`); submit once in Search Console; check robots.txt and stray
noindex tags after any redesign (his client's traffic went to zero from a forgotten blocker); request
indexing after substantial edits, not for small tweaks. "Every time Google visits a website it costs them
money … if you save Google money you will be rewarded."

**Technical floor `[7R 2]` `[CHG 3]`.** LCP under 2.5 s, CLS under 0.1, mobile-first, no broken links,
compressed images, caching, unused plugins removed; pop-ups only on exit intent `[NB2 3]`.
**ADDED/corrected:** the interaction metric is INP (good ≤ 200 ms) since March 2024, not the 100 ms he
quotes (that was FID); Google's Mobile-Friendly Test he recommends was retired in December 2023 — use
Lighthouse/PageSpeed Insights.

## 6. Re-optimising — two procedures

### 6.1 The page-two method (positions 11–20), nine steps `[P2]`

1. Search Console → Performance → Pages → filter average position 11–20. New site with no data: take a
   competitor's 11–20 keywords instead.
2. Cluster: open the page's queries; add People-also-ask and related searches; several 50–100-search
   terms on one page add up to 500–1,000. Works for product pages too.
3. Three kings (4.1).
4. URL, meta description, alt text.
5. Study the top three; a prompt: "Analyze these competitor articles for [keyword] and tell me what's
   missing, what's outdated, and how I can create a better piece of content."
6. Request indexing; **track every cluster keyword**, not only the main one.
7. If not top-10 after 4–6 weeks: mobile speed → 3–5 internal links with the keyword → indexing issues →
   a few relevant backlinks.

His claim behind it: position 10 gets 2–3% of clicks, position 3 gets 20–30% `[P2 0]`. **TEST:** the
direction is right, the figures are generous — published click curves put position 3 near 10% and
position 1 near 27–40% (he himself quotes 28% for position 1 in `[CHG 1]`). Do not promise "10×".

### 6.2 "Why Doesn't This Rank?" — the weekly diagnostic `[WDR]`

Run on one page for one keyword, in this order:

1. **Is it the right keyword?** Volume and difficulty (480 / KD 18 "I love to see"). Check the variant
   actually used on the page — maybe that one is the better target.
2. **Are links the reason?** Referring domains of each page-one result. If several have 0–6, links are
   not the blocker.
3. **Keyword use:** count on our page vs the #1 (7 vs 19–23); is it in title, meta, URL, H1, H2s, H3?
4. **Length:** 1,300 vs 2,100 words → bring it level.
5. **Structure and furniture:** table of contents, infographic, readable heading styles, column width,
   video inside the content and not behind a consent click that hides it from Google.
6. **Internal/external links:** anchors consistent; sources cited; other pages link here with the exact
   keyword.
7. **Author/E-E-A-T:** author page with profiles.
8. **Google Business Profile:** link to it with the CID link; answer the page's questions in the profile.
9. **Findability:** is the post reachable from the blog index, or buried past the pagination?
10. **Backlinks to the page:** if zero, get two or three.

Verdict format he gives the client: "You're not using the keyword enough, not long enough, don't have
backlinks, content could be better."

When a tracked keyword drops (his example: position 6 → 27): "our content isn't good … this is a good
sign to go in and refresh" — add experience, expertise, case studies; look at who is now 1–3 `[SEM 10]`.

## 7. Search Console → Looker Studio: the report `[LS]`

**Why.** "What gets measured gets optimised." Four metrics, "you really only need two": **clicks and
impressions**. Click-through rate falling while clicks and impressions rise is not a problem; average
position is shown for the client, not used — a post ranks for many keywords and the average means little
`[LS 5]`. Avoid "paralysis by analysis" `[LS 1]` `[SEM 42]`.

**The report's pages:**

| Page | What it shows | Use |
|---|---|---|
| Cover | Client, image | — |
| Activity report | Everything done this month: competitive analysis, keyword research, N posts published (linked), backlinks (linked), pages optimised (linked) | Sent also as PDF "for their records" |
| High-level metrics | Impressions, clicks, CTR, position; top pages; top queries; device split; trends | Overview |
| Year over year | Clicks and impressions, this month vs same month last year | "The slide I like best" — growth |
| Keyword 1, Keyword 2 … | All queries **containing a product word** (e.g. "sauna", "cold") with the four metrics | One page per business line |
| Month blog tracking (one per month) | Only the posts published or pages updated **that month**, tracked forever; green/red change colours | Cohort: what we did in June, how it is doing now |

## 8. Ranking on LLMs (ChatGPT, Perplexity, Google AI Mode) `[LLM]` `[END]` `[JUN]` `[CHG]`

### 8.1 His position

"LLMO … that's the stupidest thing I've ever heard." You do not need "something wildly different"
`[LLM 0]`. Test, not theory: run the same query in ChatGPT and in Google and compare.

### 8.2 What his tests showed

- **Best pizza Beamsville:** ChatGPT's list matched Google's map pack, Bing and TripAdvisor almost one
  for one; order differed; the top pick was not the one with the most reviews or best rating `[END 1-2]`
  `[LLM 37-39]`.
- **Antique shops, Perth:** the first names matched Google's map pack; ChatGPT also surfaced a shop with
  **one review** and no "antique" on its site — because directories listed it under that category. Its
  sources were MapQuest, a "top 10 best antique stores" page, a Facebook page (the business had no
  website) `[LLM 14-21]`.
- **Caricaturists near me:** results did not match Google; nearly all came from **one directory**
  (heard as "eventive.com" — probably Eventective, name unchecked; he looked it up: authority 61,
  ~800,000 visits a month). The business with 21 source
  references led those with 9 and 6 `[LLM 28-36]`.
- Sources ChatGPT showed in his tests: TripAdvisor, Yelp, Reddit, MapQuest, the business's own site, its
  Facebook page, and a **TikTok review** `[LLM 39]`.

His conclusion: "ChatGPT is crowdsourcing the information." It takes what Google and Bing show, plus
directories, lists and social posts; a **mention counts without a link** `[LLM 18, 34, 40]`. Category
matters: the Google Business Profile category, the word in the business name, and "how other people are
categorising it" `[LLM 22-24]`.

### 8.3 The procedure (per client, per money query)

1. Ask the assistant the query the customer would ask; ask Google the short version.
2. Write down who is recommended and **open every cited source**.
3. Count each business's source references; note which directory or list supplies most of the answer.
4. Check that source's weight (authority, traffic) — "this is 100% something that I would do" `[LLM 33]`.
5. Worklist: get listed on that directory/list; then the others (Yelp, TripAdvisor, chamber of commerce,
   local papers, Google Business Profile, Bing); get reviews, photos, check-ins, tags; "do influencer
   events" if you have premises; people posting and geotagging you `[LLM 19, 41]`.
6. On the site: describe services in detail and answer real questions; key takeaways at the top; answer
   in the first 150 words `[LLM 41]` `[JUN 1]` `[CHG 1]`.
7. Do not block AI crawlers — Cloudflare blocks them by default `[JUN 2]`.
8. Captions on Instagram/TikTok with the keywords, written naturally: Instagram posts are shown in Google
   results `[NB1 14]` `[JUN 2]`.
9. Keep doing the core: two posts a week, four links a month `[END 3]`.

Also: **llms.txt** — he switched it on (Yoast Premium → Settings → bottom) "just in case", but ChatGPT
could not read his own file or even his sitemap in the test; "I don't think it's going to have a huge
impact"; the `.md` page-twin idea did not work in Yoast `[LLM 1-9]`. **ChatGPT product discovery** — a
sign-up for product feed submission existed; not open to him `[LLM 9-11]`. Branded searches get 18% more
clicks with AI overviews, and AI overviews appear less on buying and "near me" queries, so target
commercial keywords `[CHG 1]`. Prediction: who publishes will matter as much as what is said; "best of"
lists and brand mentions will matter more `[JUN 3]`.

### 8.4 TEST and ADDED

- **llms.txt — he was right.** Ahrefs (May 2026, 137,210 domains): 97% of llms.txt files received zero
  requests; SE Ranking found no correlation with AI citations; Google's AI-optimisation guide of
  2026-05-15 lists it among things a site does not need
  ([Ahrefs](https://ahrefs.com/blog/llmstxt-study/),
  [Search Engine Land](https://searchengineland.com/does-llms-txt-matter-467740),
  [Optimizely](https://www.optimizely.com/field-notes/articles/does-llms-txt-work/)). Cost of having one is
  near zero; it is not a deliverable to sell.
- **"If you rank on Google you show up in ChatGPT" is too strong** outside simple local queries. Ahrefs
  (August 2025, 15,000 long-tail prompts): only ~12% of URLs cited by assistants rank in Google's top 10
  for the same prompt; ChatGPT ~8%; Perplexity closest to search rankings — because assistants fan a
  prompt out into several searches ([Ahrefs](https://ahrefs.com/blog/ai-search-overlap/)). His own
  caricaturist test shows the same thing. So the durable part of his method is not the conclusion but the
  **procedure**: read the answer's sources query by query and get the client into them. That procedure is
  what we automate.
- **Cloudflare** has blocked AI crawlers by default on new zones since July 2025 — correct, and a real
  check for every client site.
- **Instagram in Google:** public posts of professional accounts became indexable on 2025-07-10 — correct.
- **Product feeds:** what he saw became OpenAI's merchant feed and in-chat checkout (announced September
  2025). For e-commerce clients this is a line of work of its own (OPEN-QUESTIONS 6).
- "Google sends 66% more traffic to websites" `[END 0]` and "AI overviews on 18% of searches / 34.5% fewer
  clicks / 47.5% desktop drop" `[CHG 0]` are third-party figures he repeats without naming the study
  (**ADDED:** the 34.5% is Ahrefs, April 2025). Do not quote them to a client as ours.
- The mention-tracking can be bought as data: DataForSEO's AI Optimization API has an LLM Mentions
  endpoint (how a brand, domain or keyword is mentioned in ChatGPT and Google AI Overview answers, with the
  top cited domains and pages) ([DataForSEO docs](https://docs.dataforseo.com/v3/ai_optimization/llm_mentions/overview/)).

## 9. "They Ask, You Answer" — Marcus Sheridan

### 9.1 What the interview gives `[MS]`

**The story (his claims).** River Pools and Spas, Virginia, founded 2001; near bankruptcy after 2008;
first post March 2009; became the most-visited swimming-pool site in the world; traffic 100,000–500,000
a month by season; ~95% of traffic and leads organic; the local business does about 150 pools and ~$15M a

homeowners demanded "a River Pool"; manufacturing sold in 2020; all dealers together "probably somewhere

that speculation.)

**The method as told:**

1. **Listen and write it down.** After each sales visit: "have I written about that on the website
   yet?" Goal: "every single question I hear in a sales appointment already answered well on my website
   through text, through video" `[MS 23]`. "If you're getting redundant questions all the time it's your
   own fault." Sit with the sales team one day → six months of ideas `[MS 24-25]`.
2. **The Big 5** — the five subjects that move traffic and sales: **cost/price, problems, comparisons
   (versus), reviews, best** `[MS 13]`.
3. **Price.** Everyone searches it; most companies refuse to discuss it. "How much does a fiberglass
   pool cost" — one article, about 30 minutes to write, "$35 million in sales that I've tracked back"
   `[MS 15]`. You need not publish the final price to discuss price.
4. **Competitors.** "Who are the best pool builders in Richmond, Virginia (reviews/ratings)": his five
   best competitors, each described, **himself not on the list** ("that sounds self-serving"). It ranked
   for "best pool builders Richmond" and for "[competitor] reviews"; over $250,000 in the first year.
   Never negative, never speculation `[MS 31-34]`.
5. **Awards.** As a dealer he published "Fiberglass Pool Design Awards 2011" — best kidney shape, best
   rectangle — to manufacturers he competed with. They were angry, then invited him to their factories.
   "What gives me the right? The internet gave me the right" `[MS 18-20]`.
6. **Plain language.** "The curse of knowledge"; never say "SEO", say "getting found online"; "it's
   dumb not to dumb it down"; "the moment you try to sound smart is the moment you start to look stupid"
   `[MS 1-6]`. He was not a pool-installation expert; he could listen and translate `[MS 21]`.
7. **Cadence and length.** At IMPACT: two pieces a week minimum, three preferred; 1,200–1,500 words;
   quantity **and** quality `[MS 28-29]`. IMPACT writes nothing: every client has an **in-house content
   person** and is coached `[MS 29]`.
8. **Titles and subheads** written exactly as people search; sloppy technical finish is the usual flaw of
   people who "read the book" `[MS 30-31]`.
9. **Time.** Two years, 10:30 pm to about 12:30 am every night after 60-hour weeks. "We don't have the
   time … in reality we just don't have the pain" `[MS 35-37]`. "The law of the harvest"; the snowball
   crests the hill; some seeds never take (he never ranked first for "fiberglass pools") `[MS 46-48]`.
10. **AI prompt he uses:** "You're an expert in Marcus Sheridan's They Ask, You Answer methodology. I
    want you to come up with at least 50 titles to blog articles focusing on the Big 5 … cost, problems,
    comparisons, reviews and best. List these exactly as somebody would search them and exactly as
    somebody would ask them" `[MS 13]`.
11. **Tools then:** his ears, AnswerThePublic, Google autocomplete with "vs" / "for" `[MS 25]`.

Matt's own proof of the same idea: "How to add users to a Meta business profile", written because every

### 9.2 What the interview only sketches — completed from Sheridan's published method (**ADDED**)

Sources: *They Ask, You Answer* (2017, revised 2019), *Endless Customers* (2025), IMPACT's learning
centre ([Selling 7](https://impactplus.com/blog/win-more-business-with-the-selling-7),
[Four Pillars](https://www.impactplus.com/blog/the-four-pillars-of-a-known-trusted-brand-marcus-sheridans-blueprint),
[Endless Customers](https://www.impactplus.com/what-is-endless-customers)).

- **The Big 5 in full.** (1) *Cost and pricing* — what drives the price up and down, the range, why some
  are cheap, where you sit. (2) *Problems* — the drawbacks of what you sell, stated by you before the buyer
  finds them ("fiberglass pool problems"). (3) *Versus and comparisons* — your thing against the
  alternative, including the one you do not sell. (4) *Reviews* — honest reviews of products, methods and
  companies in your space. (5) *Best in class* — "best X for Y" lists, competitors included.
- **The principle under it:** buyers do most of the decision before talking to anyone; the company
  willing to answer what others hide becomes the trusted one. Honesty includes saying **who you are not a
  good fit for** — it raises trust and filters bad leads.
- **Assignment selling.** Sales uses the content: before a meeting the prospect is sent the specific
  article or video that answers their questions, and is asked to read it. Sheridan's measured finding at
  River Pools: prospects who read 30 or more pages before the appointment closed at about 80%. Content is
  a sales tool first, a traffic tool second.
- **The Selling 7** — seven videos that shorten sales: 80% videos (the questions asked in 80% of sales
  calls, one video each), bio videos of the people the buyer will meet, product/service fit ("who this is
  and is not for"), landing-page videos (what happens after you fill in this form), cost and pricing,
  customer journey (a real customer's before–during–after), "claims we make" (proof of each claim).
- **Roles.** A full-time **content manager** inside the company (publishing 2–3 pieces a week), a
  videographer, and sales people as the subject experts who are interviewed. Leadership buy-in via a
  company workshop. This is why the interview-first drafting of section 4.4 is the same method: the
  expert talks, the content person writes.
- **Self-service tools.** Price estimators, configurators and self-assessment quizzes on the site: the
  buyer answers their own question and becomes a lead with their data attached.
- **Endless Customers (2025) — the Four Pillars of a known and trusted brand:** say what others in your
  industry are not willing to say; show what others are not willing to show (video); sell in a way others
  are not willing to sell (self-service, transparency); be more human than others are willing to be. The
  book moves the method beyond the website: the same answers on YouTube, social, podcasts — because
  search is no longer only Google. That is where it meets section 8.

### 9.3 TEST

- The method is sound and has outlived every update because it is Google's own stated aim; nothing in it
  is a trick. Evidence quality: the $35M and $250K figures are Sheridan's own attribution; the "$100M" is
  a guess he labels as a guess.
- "Best of" lists and awards about competitors are legitimate only if they are honest and the criteria
  are stated. A list rigged to capture a rival's brand searches while hiding the author's interest would be
  deception. Sheridan's own version — himself off the list, nothing negative — is the sound form.
- What it costs is what people skip: an expert who talks every week, for two years.

## 10. Instagram virality — the full strategy `[IG]` `[SFV]` `[STORY]` `[MS]`

He states he sells nothing about Instagram growth `[IG 0, 44]`. The session is a live workshop rewriting a
viewer's 1 min 40 s hair-straightener review into a 15-second script.

### 10.1 Targets (his numbers)

| Metric | Target | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Video length | 30–45 s; his best were 20–31 s; one 60 s video hit a million "but that never happened again" | `[IG 4]` |
| Average view | at least 50% of the video — "the golden ratio" | `[IG 5]` |
| Average watch time | 15–20 s (his 38 s video: 14 s — "not the worst and not the best") | `[IG 5, 43]` |
| Interactions | ~10% of plays (17,999 plays → 1,827 interactions: ~1,100 saves, 500 likes, 201 shares, 29 comments, 10 follows) | `[IG 42]` |
| Script length | 5–6 lines; a 6-line script timed at 14–15 s | `[IG 4, 36]` |
| Posting | 1–3 a day, spaced; not 20 | `[IG 54-55]` |

**Signal order, as he reads it:** follows from the video (the top signal) → shares → saves → comments →
likes. He checks new followers from a video after a couple of hours `[IG 40-41]`. Under ~6 seconds a video
is not pushed even at high watch percentage `[IG 9]`. Platforms want time on platform; help them.

### 10.2 The hook

- **About the viewer, not you.** "I think I found the best straightener for my travel" fails: it is
  about her `[IG 1]`.
- **Short, no spare words.** "This is the best straightener for travel." Drop "hair" if you are holding
  one `[IG 6]`. Four or five words is the feel `[IG 15]`.
- **A promise that the video pays later** — "why is it the best? I need to watch more" `[IG 11, 14]`.
- **Not infomercial.** "Do you hate when your hair gets frizzy? … I've got the product for you" — no.
  "We should never be selling; we should always be educating and entertaining" `[IG 7]`.
- **Not the whole story.** "I was so frustrated … I got this straightener and it solved it" gives
  everything away `[IG 14]`.
- **Intrigue works:** "Don't tell Google that I told you this, but …" `[IG 14]` (that video: 1.7M views
  `[IG 63]`).
- **Not too narrow:** "Flying to Chicago?" loses everyone not flying to Chicago; "overseas" keeps them
  `[IG 10, 20]`.
- **Choose by ear:** read the candidates aloud as if filming; take the one that "rolls off your tongue"
  `[IG 16]`.
- Humility rule: "some of the hooks my wife uses I'm like nobody would watch that — and she has a million
  views" `[IG 12]`. Hooks are tested, not known.

### 10.3 Hook stacking and the script

- **Hook stacking:** after hook one, a second line that keeps them ("… especially if you're traveling
  overseas"), and a **visual hook at the same moment** — the action that proves the line (putting it in a
  purse on "it fits in your purse") `[IG 8, 23-24]`.
- **One short sentence per line — "like a poem"**; "everything we have here should be necessary to tell
  the story" `[IG 21-22]`.
- **Self-edit while writing:** "I had to try it myself" → "I had to try it" `[IG 32]`. A word the viewer
  already has (travel, said twice) is not said again `[IG 52]`.
- **Read it aloud about 30 times** while writing; if a line does not flow spoken, rewrite it even if it
  reads well on paper `[IG 21, 46]`.
- **Move the payoff down:** the second feature ("and it curls your hair too") moved from the opening to
  after the result — "this is my hair after only five minutes — and it curls your hair too" `[IG 58-60]`.
- **Tease the name.** Show it, describe it, do not name it; name it at the end. The hook's promise
  **must** be paid ("otherwise they're going to be pissed") `[IG 30, 65]`.
- **Cut what is not value:** heat settings, anti-scald bristles, the "so what tool is this?" question —
  out, or into the caption `[IG 33, 48, 68]`.
- **Borrowed proof without selling:** "I saw this on TikTok and I had to try it" `[IG 31]`; "not
  sponsored, I bought this" in the caption; "I'm not affiliated with this company so I can trash them"
  `[IG 59-60]`.

The finished script (15 s):
> This is the best straightener for travel, / especially if you're traveling overseas. / It's so small
> that it fits in your purse / and you don't even have to plug it in. / I saw this on TikTok and I had
> to try it. / Guys, this is my hair after only five minutes — / and it curls your hair too. / It's
> called the [name] and I'm in love. / Comment [word] and I'll DM you the link.

### 10.4 Filming and editing

Film line by line: read one or two lines off the phone, say them, next; cut together fast with no dead air
— "boom, fact, fact … they don't have a chance to click off" `[IG 22-23]`. Change the script while
filming if it does not flow; add a human filler where it helps `[IG 25]`. Green screen: yourself small in
the corner talking over footage or screenshots `[IG 64]`. Light matters (he turns on a second light
mid-session) `[IG 34]`. Text on screen pointing to the caption ("curl settings in the caption") `[IG 45]`.
Cost: a phone and free apps; long-form kit about $4,000 `[SFV 5-6]`.

### 10.5 Caption and call to action

- **Long caption for the detail.** "If you want the full details go in my caption" — they get the value
  and "the video is still playing in the background as they're reading … that increases your watch time"
  `[IG 38-39]`. Write captions with the keywords you want to be found for, naturally `[NB1 14]` `[JUN 2]`.
- **Comment-to-DM.** "Comment [word] and I'll DM you a link" at the **end** — those who reach the end are
  the engaged ones. Run by ManyChat (sign in through Facebook) `[IG 71-73]`.
- **Send people where the money is.** Not to the blog (AdSense "sucks": his 4M-visit site would make
  about $4K on AdSense); to the affiliate storefront, the list, the offer `[IG 69-72]`.
- **TikTok differs:** no DM automation; he says he was permanently banned (later reversed) for repeating
  "click the link in my bio". His workaround: show a screenshot of the profile with the link circled and
  say "go here and grab [thing]" `[IG 73-75]`.
