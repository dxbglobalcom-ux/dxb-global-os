# Hook evidence — BOOKS and tested masters (slice "kitap")

Gathered, not judged. Grades follow the brief (A field/A-B archive, B large observational, C lab, D platform data, E practitioner with counted data, F experience). Local extracts (full text I opened) are in this folder: `sa.txt` (Hopkins, Scientific Advertising), `mlia.txt` (My Life in Advertising), `tam.txt` (Caples, Tested Advertising Methods, 5th ed. 1997, rev. Fred Hahn), `bt.txt` (Schwartz), `cash.txt` (Whitman), `mts.txt` (Heath), `og.txt` (Ogilvy, Confessions ch. VI), `sug.html` (Sugarman), `mrb.txt` (MrBeast document), `bm.txt` (Berger & Milkman). "tam.txt:1753" = line 1753 of that extract.

Important caveat for the whole slice: the old direct-response books give split-run results as ratios WITHOUT N or significance. Their design is A (randomised alternate-run, behaviour = replies/calls/coupons), but the reader cannot check size, so I weight them as E until a modern replication exists. The Upworthy archive (section d) is the way to re-test them with N.

## 1. Adding two words to a bare headline raised sample requests 27 %
1. **Finding.** "Hay Fever" got 297 sample requests; "Dry Up Hay Fever" got 380: a promised benefit beats a bare topic.
2. Caples, *Tested Advertising Methods*, 5th ed., Prentice Hall 1997 (rev. Hahn), ch. on mass-market appeals, pp. 194-195. https://archive.org/details/tested-advertising-methods (opened, full text).
3. **Proof: A by design (newspaper split-run), E by checkability.** Measured: sample requests (clicking-equivalent, not buying). 297 vs 380 = +27 %. N of readers not given.
4. Holds: newspaper, 1940s-90s, US, a drug/remedy with a free-sample offer. No replication found by me.
5. **Hook.** Pattern: topic word + action/benefit verb. Olive oil: "Olive oil" → "Cook Healthier Tonight With Olive Oil". Channel: headline / on-screen text / caption first line.
6. Quote: "The ad with the headline 'Hay Fever' pulled 297 sample requests. The ad with the headline 'Dry Up Hay Fever' pulled 380 sample requests. This is a 27 percent increase obtained by merely adding two words." Read: section (tam.txt:12982).

## 2. Naming the buyer in the first words ("Car owners!") raised replies 20 %
1. **Finding.** Putting the audience label first pulled 20 % more sample requests with nothing else changed.
2. Caples, same book, "How to make a good headline better" (≈p.194) and again in the split-run chapter (ch. 17 "Seventeen Ways to Test Your Advertising"). Same URL.
3. **Proof: A by design, N not given.** Ad A "Save one gallon of gas in every ten"; Ad B "Car Owners! Save one gallon of gas in every ten"; B +20 % replies (requests for a sample). Same test quoted twice in the book, so it is one test, not two.
4. Holds: daily newspaper, gasoline additive. Hopkins (1923) and Ogilvy (1963) give the same rule without numbers: "Hey there, Bill Jones" (Hopkins ch.5, sa.txt:1267); display BLADDER WEAKNESS / MOTHERS (Ogilvy ch. VI, og.txt:83). Hopkins' warning: a clever blind headline attracts many readers "mostly impossible subjects" (sa.txt:1289).
5. **Hook.** Dentist: "Parents of children under 10: a free check that finds cavities before they hurt". Furniture: "Small-flat owners: a sofa that fits through a 70 cm door". Channel: headline, first spoken line, first on-screen words.
6. Quote: "Ad B pulled 20 percent more replies." Read: section (tam.txt:12950-12965, 17010).

## 3. A concrete benefit beat a "philosophical" opening 2.5 to 1; a cooling promise beat a humidity promise 2.5 to 1
1. **Finding.** "How you can get a loan of $200" brought 2.5 times the calls of "When can a loan help you?"; "How to have a cool, quiet bedroom — even on hot nights" brought 2.5 times the calls of "Get rid of that humidity".
2. Caples, ch. 17, pp. 250-251 (page markers in tam.txt:16258-16313).
3. **Proof: A by design, E by checkability.** Measured: telephone calls, each ad credited by a different name ("ask for Miss Miller / Miss Johnson"); alternate-week newspaper runs, half-million circulation for the air-conditioner test. N of calls not given.
4. Holds: US newspaper, finance company and air-conditioning. Same logic as Hopkins' soap/automobile examples (sa.txt:1452-1470; Hopkins says "might outpull ... fifty to one": a hypothetical, not a result).
5. **Hook.** The question "what do they get/avoid?" in the first line, not the mood. Software: "How to close your books in 2 days" over "Is your finance process holding you back?". Channel: headline / email subject / first spoken line.
6. Quote: "'How you can get a loan of $200' brought two and a half times as many telephone calls as the other ad." Read: section.

## 4. One offer beat nine offers 3 to 1
1. **Finding.** An ad showing ONE watch outsold the identical ad showing nine styles three to one.
2. Joseph Sugarman, *The Adweek Copywriting Handbook*, 2007, pp. 173-175. https://archive.org/stream/the-adweek-copywriting-handbook/The%20Adweek%20Copywriting%20handbook_djvu.txt (opened, full text).
3. **Proof: A by design (A/B split in the Wall Street Journal), E by checkability.** Measured: sales (buying) from the ad. 3:1. N not given. Sugarman also repeats Caples' "repair → fix, +20 %" one-word result (sug.html:6818) as hearsay: UNVERIFIED against Caples.
4. Holds: one mail-order ad, one product, 1980s US. Sugarman notes the full range was shown later in the catalog to people already qualified.
5. **Hook.** First frame shows one thing and one promise. Furniture: one chair on a white floor, not a showroom. Channel: visual / thumbnail.
6. Quote: "the ad that featured only one men's watch outpulled the other version that featured nine models by a surprising 3 to 1 ratio." Read: section.

## 5. Small layout/picture choices moved replies 5-25 %; destination photos beat aircraft photos 2 to 1
1. **Finding.** A smiling model beat a serious one (+25 % replies); headline under the picture beat over it (+8 %); KLM destination photos pulled twice the coupons of aircraft photos.
2. Caples ch. 17 (tam.txt:16990-17020) and Ogilvy, *Confessions of an Advertising Man* (1963), ch. VI "How to Write Potent Copy", section on illustrations (og.txt:205). Ogilvy text: https://studfile.net/preview/3742133/page:2/ (Russian host, English text; section read).
3. **Proof: A by design, N not given.** Measured: replies/coupons. +25 %, +8 %, 2x. Caples' two ads are anonymous "beauty product" and "drug product".
4. Holds: print mail-order, 1960s-90s. No video/short-form replication.
5. **Hook.** Visual: a smiling face (eyes to camera) over a neutral face; show where the customer GOES (the view, the finished kitchen) not the vehicle (the plane, the oven). Channel: visual / thumbnail.
6. Quote: "The latter pulled twice as many coupons as the former." Read: section.

## 6. Headline change multiplies returns 5-10x; cost per reply ranged $0.41 to $14.20 for a $5 item
1. **Finding.** On the same product the same body copy under different headlines varied 5-10 times; the best and worst ad differed ~35 times in cost per reply.
2. Claude Hopkins, *Scientific Advertising* (1923), ch. 4 "Mail Order Advertising" and ch. 5 "Headlines". https://archive.org/details/scientific-advertising (opened, full text; sa.txt:912-921, 1392-1418).
3. **Proof: E (counted, keyed coupons; no N).** Measured: replies and cost per reply (clicking-equivalent). "$14.20 vs 85 cents vs 41 cents" per reply; "five to ten times"; "keyed returns on nearly two thousand headlines used on a single product". Ogilvy gives the same "ten to one" (og.txt:82).
4. Holds: 1920s US mail order. Hopkins says "the appeals we like best will rarely prove best" (sa.txt:1478).
5. **Hook.** Rule for a hook library: store every hook WITH its measured result; never trust the author's taste. Channel: all.
6. Quote: "It is not uncommon for a change in headlines to multiply returns from five to ten times over." Read: whole chapters 4-5.

## 7. "Because + any reason" lifted compliance 60 % → 93-94 % (1978), but failed to replicate (1985)
1. **Finding.** Adding "because" plus a (weak) reason made queue-jumpers succeed far more often, though a later set of four studies could not reproduce it.
2. Langer, Blank, Chanowitz, *J. Personality & Social Psychology* 36(6), 1978, https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.36.6.635 (abstract opened; numbers 60/93/94 % from Psychology Today summary https://www.psychologytoday.com/us/blog/brain-wise/201310/the-power-of-the-word-because-to-get-people-to-do-stuff, **UNVERIFIED against the paper**). Replication attempt: Folkes, JPSP 48(3), 1985, https://doi.org/10.1037//0022-3514.48.3.600 (abstract opened).
3. **Proof: A (field experiment, three studies) but contested.** Behaviour: letting someone use the copier. 60 % → 93 % ("because I have to make copies") and 94 % ("because I'm in a rush") for a 5-page request; N not read. Folkes: "Neither study replicated Langer et al.'s results" (4 studies, conclusion: cognitive processing rather than mindlessness).
4. Holds: US campus copier line, small request only. The original also ran a 20-page request; its result I did not read. Not a purchase finding.
5. **Hook.** A reason in the first sentence ("...because the price changes Friday"). Use as a hypothesis to A/B, not as a rule. Channel: spoken line / caption.
6. Quote (Folkes): "Neither study replicated Langer ct al.'s results." Read: abstract only (both).

## 8. Telling people what the majority does: a field win (2008) and a failed replication (2014)
1. **Finding.** Hotel signs saying "most guests reuse their towels" beat the usual environmental sign in the US; a German replication found no advantage over the standard sign.
2. Goldstein, Cialdini, Griskevicius, *JCR* 35(3), 2008, https://academic.oup.com/jcr/article-abstract/35/3/472/1856257. Replication: Bohner & Schlüter, *PLOS ONE* 9(8), 2014, https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0104086.
3. **Proof: A both; the replication has N = 724 (hotel 1) and 204 (hotel 2).** Behaviour: towels reused. Goldstein effect sizes: not verified (abstract only; my search query's 35/44/49 % numbers were not in any page I opened: UNVERIFIED). Bohner: "descriptive norm messages were not more effective than the standard message".
4. Holds: US hotel 2008; fails/inconsistent in two German hotels. Cialdini's *Influence* / *Pre-Suasion* carry the 2008 result without the failure (books not opened).
5. **Hook.** Social proof as a first line is a hypothesis, not a law: "Most owners in your street choose..." only after an A/B. Channel: headline / on-screen.
6. Quote (Bohner): "both standard and descriptive norm messages increased reuse rates compared to a no-message baseline. However, descriptive norm messages were not more effective than the standard message". Read: abstract only both.

## 9. Emotion with high arousal gets shared more (N = 6,956 articles)
1. **Finding.** News stories that made readers feel awe or anger were far likelier to reach the most-emailed list; sadness made them less likely.
2. Berger & Milkman, "What Makes Online Content Viral?", *J. Marketing Research* 49(2), 2012. http://jonahberger.com/wp-content/uploads/2013/02/ViralityB.pdf (opened PDF; bm.txt:486-505).
3. **Proof: B.** N = 6,956 NYT articles (30 Aug-30 Nov 2008). Measured: SHARING (most-e-mailed list), not stopping. Change in probability per +1 SD: anger +34 %, awe +30 %, interest +25 %, anxiety +21 %, emotionality +18 %, surprise +14 %, positivity +13 %, sadness -16 %.
4. Holds: one newspaper, US, 2008; observational (headline vs article not separated). Related *Contagious*/*Magic Words* books not opened.
5. **Hook.** Aim for a high-arousal emotion in the first words, not a calm one. Dentist: "The thing your dentist notices that you can't" (alarm/curiosity) over "Keeping teeth healthy". Channel: headline / caption.
6. Quote: "Content that evokes high-arousal positive (awe) or negative (anger or anxiety) emotions is more viral." Read: whole.

## 10. Surprise and joy hold viewers in an ad; brand-in-the-middle of the screen makes them zap
1. **Finding.** People stop zapping an ad when it surprises or amuses them; a brand sitting in the centre of the picture made viewers zap sooner; showing the brand in pulses beat constant showing.
2. Teixeira, Wedel, Pieters, "Emotion-Induced Engagement in Internet Video Advertisements", *JMR* 2012, http://www.economicsofattention.com/site/assets/files/1054/emotion-induced_engagement-_teixeira_et_al-_jmr.pdf (opened). Teixeira, Wedel, Pieters, "Moment-to-moment optimal branding in TV commercials: preventing avoidance by pulsing", *Marketing Science* 2010, https://www.hbs.edu/faculty/Pages/item.aspx?num=37527 (abstract).
3. **Proof: C (lab with behaviour = zapping).** 2012: 50 viewers x 28 ads, 145,000 frames at 250 ms; 2010: ~2,000 participants, 31 commercials plus a controlled experiment on edited ads. Measured: continued watching/zapping, eye tracking, facial expression. Effect sizes not extracted.
4. Holds: in-lab web browsing, students/staff, ads of 2000s; not a feed.
5. **Hook.** Put the surprise/smile in the first seconds, not the logo. Visual/first spoken line.
6. Quote: "Surprise and joy effectively concentrate attention and retain viewers." Read: 2012 section; 2010 abstract only.

## 11. Entertainment BEFORE the brand did not add to purchase intent; entertainment AFTER it did
1. **Finding.** A funny first half that comes before the brand does not help buying; fun that comes after the brand shows does.
2. Teixeira, Picard, el Kaliouby, *Marketing Science* 33(6), 2014. https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/10.1287/mksc.2014.0854 (abstract opened).
3. **Proof: C-B.** 82 ads, 178 consumers in homes/workplaces; measured smile, viewing interest, stated PURCHASE INTENT (not purchase). "Inverted U" between entertainment and intent.
4. Holds: TV-style ads shown on the web, 2014. A tension with the usual "hook first, brand later" (see contradictions).
5. **Hook.** Don't spend the hook on a gag unrelated to the product. Channel: visual/spoken.
6. Quote: "the latter is positively associated with purchase intent while the former is not." Read: abstract only.

## 12. Show the brand early and often, with one spoken mention (recall, not purchase)
1. **Finding.** Branding early and several times plus one verbal mention improves remembering who the ad was for; a brand merely on screen for long does not.
2. Romaniuk (Ehrenberg-Bass), "The Efficacy of Brand-Execution Tactics in TV Advertising, Brand Placements, and Internet Advertising", *J. Advertising Research* 49(2), 2009, pp. 143-150. https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.2501/S0021849909090187 (abstract opened).
3. **Proof: C.** "Multiple studies" (N not read). Measured: brand recall.
4. Holds: TV/internet video, 2009. Also read next to #11.
5. **Hook.** Say or show the shop's name inside the first 3-5 seconds, once spoken. Channel: spoken + on-screen.
6. Quote: "showing the brand early and often—and having at least one verbal mention—enhances brand recall." Read: abstract only.

## 13. Two seconds of real attention is a threshold for sales effect; memory starts near 3 s
1. **Finding.** Ads that got more than 2 s of active gaze showed higher sales uplift and decayed slower; each active second ≈ 3 days in memory.
2. Karen Nelson-Field (Amplified Intelligence), "More proof of the value of attention: Evidence from DACH", Nov 2020 slide deck. https://screenforce.nl/wp-content/uploads/2020/11/Slidedeck-Online-Talk-Karen-Nelson-Field.pdf (opened). *The Attention Economy* (2020) itself not opened.
3. **Proof: D (vendor's own data; peer review absent).** N not stated in the deck. Active-attention seconds vs short-term sales uplift (STAS): r = .82, p < .001; platform index vs attention r = .97 on 5 platforms (Facebook 100, Instagram 105, YouTube 112, TV 129, BVOD 138). Above-2 s vs below-2 s: STAS +5 to +11 points in 10 of 12 country-platform rows. Measured: gaze (AI) and sales uplift via product choice.
4. Holds: Germany/Switzerland/Austria 2020, TV/BVOD/YouTube/Instagram/Facebook. Confirms (not contradicts) the "3 seconds" platform claims.
5. **Hook.** The first 2-3 s decide whether any later effect can exist. Channel: all video.
6. Quote: "2 seconds of attention is not enough, and no platform is immune to this reality." (slide text: "Memory starts to kick in at around the 3 second mark."). Read: whole deck.

## 14. Ads built from human, funny, character-driven features rate higher (System1)
1. **Finding.** Among 200 TV ads, those with the fewest "right-brain" features (faces, humour, characters, music, flow) almost never earned a top emotional rating.
2. Orlando Wood, *Lemon* (IPA 2019) as reported by System1 / LBB. https://lbbonline.com/news/are-ads-becoming-too-far-left-brain (opened). *Look Out* press release https://system1group.com/blog/look-out-press-release (opened). Books not opened.
3. **Proof: D/C.** 100 UK + 100 US TV ads, System1 star rating (emotional response, "predictive of market share growth"); >60 % of the fewest-feature ads got 1 star; 5 % of the most-feature ads got 5 stars vs 0 %. Measured: self-reported emotion, NOT behaviour. The vendor sells the rating.
4. Holds: UK/US TV; YouTube ads have 4.1 left-brain vs 1.7 right-brain features per ad.
5. **Hook.** A person, a face, a bit of humour, an unfolding scene in the first frame. Visual.
6. Quote: "More than 60% of ads using the fewest right-brained features achieved just 1-Star". Read: section (secondary).

## 15. MrBeast production document: the first minute loses the most viewers; title/thumbnail set the promise
1. **Finding.** On one 60-million-click video, 21 million (35 %) left in the first minute; the thumbnail and title promise must be met in that minute, and more extreme wording ("I Survived") lifts clicks.
2. "How to Succeed in MrBeast Production" (leaked, Aug 2024), PDF https://sambharia.com/assets/mrbeast-production-memo.pdf (opened; mrb.txt:136-195). Authenticity not confirmed by the company (Creator Handbook, 19 Sep 2024).
3. **Proof: E.** One video, counted: "60 million people that clicked", "we lost 21 million viewers in the first minute", "39 million at the 1 minute mark". CTR example: "100,000,000 see ... 10,000,000 click = 10 %". Measured: CTR, retention. The titles "I Survived" > "I Spent" and "ketchup" > "front yard" are asserted, not shown with numbers ("easily 100x more viral").
4. Holds: YouTube long-form, one creator, 2024. His own text: "the first minute has the most loss (go look)".
5. **Hook.** Thumbnail = an image that is itself the surprise; title = the extreme claim; the first minute delivers exactly that. Furniture: "I Slept On 5 Mattresses, One Broke My Back" with the five mattresses as the thumbnail. Channel: thumbnail / title / first 60 s.
6. Quote: "As with almost every video on Youtube, the first minute has the most loss". Read: section (hooks, CTR, AVD).

## 16. Short headlines are READ more (Rudolph, 2,500 ads); long headlines with news SELL more (NYU test)
1. **Finding.** Two old studies disagree on length: the shorter the headline the more people read all of it, yet headlines of 10+ words with news and information sold more goods in a store test.
2. Rudolph's study (1939-40, Saturday Evening Post, 2,500 ads) as given in Whitman, *Cashvertising* (2009), https://archive.org/details/cashvertising-how-to-use-more-than-100-secrets-of-ad-agency (cash.txt:4205-4225; OCR lost the table, so the numbers are not shown; the text says shortest ≈ 1/7 better than longest, biggest drop above 12 words). NYU School of Retailing test, in Ogilvy, *Confessions*, ch. VI (og.txt:99). Caples (tam.txt:496): "Long headlines that say something are more effective than short headlines that say nothing."
3. **Proof: B (Rudolph, N 2,500 ads, READING of whole headline) vs E (NYU, sales, N not given).** Readership vs sales are different outcomes. Primary papers not opened.
4. Holds: 1940s print. Whitman himself resolves: biggest benefit first; if two are equal use the shorter.
5. **Hook.** Don't cut a benefit to be short; cut empty words. Channel: headline / on-screen text.
6. Quote (Ogilvy): "headlines of ten words or longer, containing news and information, consistently sold more merchandise than short headlines." Read: section; secondary for Rudolph.

## 17. Self-interest > news > curiosity (Caples), and a curiosity gap needs a stated gap (Loewenstein)
1. **Finding.** Among keyed headlines, benefit headlines pulled best, news second, curiosity third, "for every curiosity headline that succeeds, a dozen will fail"; the curiosity theory says a gap must be opened first by naming what the reader lacks.
2. Caples ch. 2 (tam.txt:1996-2025) ; Heath & Heath, *Made to Stick* (2007), "Unexpected" chapter, citing Loewenstein 1994 (mts.txt:3994-4040) https://archive.org/details/made-to-stick-pdf_202303.
3. **Proof: E (Caples: "advertisers who work with keyed copy find..."; no counts) + theory/C (Loewenstein; Lowry & Johnson classroom study: 18 % of consensus students vs 45 % of disagreement students stayed in recess for a film, N not given in the book).** Measured: choosing to read/stay.
4. Holds: print mail-order; the "How a fool stunt made me a star salesman" ad combined curiosity + self-interest (tam.txt:2262-2305) and ran for years.
5. **Hook.** Curiosity + a benefit in one line. Software: "The one setting that cut our onboarding time by half". Channel: headline / subject / first spoken line.
6. Quote: "For every curiosity headline that succeeds in getting results, a dozen will fail." Read: section both.

## 18. Look-like-editorial raises readers ~50 %; useful advice "hooks 75 % more readers"; captions out-read body copy
1. **Finding.** Ads laid out like editorial pages draw about 50 % more readers; copy giving the reader helpful advice hooks about 75 % more readers than product-only copy; more people read picture captions than body copy.
2. Ogilvy, *Confessions of an Advertising Man*, ch. VI (og.txt:142, 235, and Caples tam.txt:9292 quoting *Ogilvy on Advertising* on captions).
3. **Proof: E (Starch/Gallup readership, N not stated).** Measured: readership (stopping/reading), not sales. Ogilvy himself warns that the Rinso advice ad was better read and remembered but "should never have run" because it lacked the selling promise.
4. Holds: 1960s print. Whitman (cash.txt:4953): "up to twice as many people read captions as body copy".
5. **Hook.** Useful-tip format ("How to remove red-wine stains") as the opener, with the brand promise still present. Channel: caption / headline / spoken first line.
6. Quote: "It hooks about 75 per cent more readers than copy which deals entirely with the product." Read: section.

## 19. Pictures raise noticing; reversed (light-on-dark) small type is harder to read
1. **Finding.** Ads with 50 % visuals were noticed 30 % more than ads with none; reversed type is read more slowly and from a shorter distance.
2. Roper Starch Worldwide 1991 (2,000 consumers, 10 US markets, 650 newspaper ads) and Holmes (J. Applied Psychology 1931), Starch, Paterson & Tinker (1932), all as reported in Whitman, *Cashvertising* (cash.txt:4685-4730, 6884-6895). Primary papers not opened.
3. **Proof: B for Roper Starch (N 2,000 x 650 ads, measured noticing/recall, not buying); C for type tests (N not given).** 50 % visuals +30 % noted; 75 % visuals +50 %; black-on-white readable at 66 inches vs 55 inches reversed; 4 % less legible after normal text, 16 % when reversed only (Paterson & Tinker).
4. Holds: 1930s lab; 1991 newspapers. Whitman's exception: reversed large headline with few words.
5. **Hook.** On-screen words: dark text on light panel or light text on a solid dark block, large, few words. Channel: on-screen text / thumbnail.
6. Quote: "Ads consisting of 50 percent visuals ... were noted (seen and recalled) 30 percent more often than ads with no visuals." Read: section, secondary.

## 20. Concrete language: customers buy more when staff speak concretely; Hopkins: "three and one-third times the light"
1. **Finding.** Specific, tangible words raise satisfaction and purchasing; a stated number is believed more than "best".
2. Packard & Berger, "How Concrete Language Shapes Customer Satisfaction", *JCR* 47(5), 2020. https://academic.oup.com/jcr/article/47/5/787/5873524 (abstract opened; body not extracted). Hopkins, *Scientific Advertising* ch. 7 (sa.txt:1885-1960).
3. **Proof: B/A (five studies, 1,000+ real consumer-employee interactions; I did not read the effect sizes).** Hopkins: E, no counts ("Say that a tungsten lamp gives more light ... three and one-third times the light and people realize that you have made tests").
4. Holds: US phone service of one apparel retailer and online experiments; spoken, not ad headlines.
5. **Hook.** Replace adjectives with a count: "3 cm thick oak, 25-year guarantee" not "premium". Channel: spoken / on-screen / caption.
6. Quote: "customers are more satisfied, willing to purchase, and purchase more when employees speak to them concretely." Read: abstract only; Hopkins section.

## 21. Message-style choices barely matter on average (1,149 studies), and "rhetorical question" has no reliable effect
1. **Finding.** Across 30 message designs (narrative, gain/loss, one/two-sided...), the mean difference in persuasion is small and the better option often loses; a 1998 meta-analysis found questions in a message add no appreciable persuasion.
2. O'Keefe & Hoeken, *Frontiers in Psychology* 2021, https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2021.664160/full (opened). Gayle, Preiss, Allen, in Allen & Preiss (eds.), *Persuasion: Advances Through Meta-analysis*, Hampton 1998 (cited by Whitman, cash.txt:3085-3105; book not opened, chapter present per Google Books table of terms).
3. **Proof: B (meta-analytic).** 1,149 studies, 280,591 participants, median mean r ≈ 0.10, prediction intervals include negatives. Measured: attitude/persuasion in research studies, not ad stopping.
4. Holds: lab and survey persuasion research; may under-represent very short first-second effects.
5. **Hook.** Treat any single style (question, story, number) as a candidate to test, never a default. Channel: all.
6. Quote: "choosing the on-average-more-effective option does not consistently confer a persuasive advantage". Read: abstract + conclusions; Gayle via Whitman only.

## 22. Click-through (inquiries) predicted sales in a 5-million-inquiry record
1. **Finding.** Ads that bring the most inquiries usually bring the most sales, so a first-step measure (stop, click) is a fair proxy, except for a giveaway-style hook.
2. Caples book (tam.txt:1745-1760), citing Daniel Starch's analysis of five million inquiries from 165 firms over 12 years. Primary Starch not opened.
3. **Proof: B, secondary.** N = 5,000,000 inquiries, 165 firms, 12 years. Measured: inquiries vs sales. Exception stated: "Send for this FREE gift" brings an avalanche of worthless leads.
4. Holds: 20th-century US industrial/ad mail-order, qualified by phone follow-up.
5. **Hook.** Judge hooks by click AND downstream result; a free-gift hook can inflate the first step. Channel: all.
6. Quote: "the advertisements that bring the most inquiries ... usually bring in the most sales." Read: section.

## 23. Reading path of a letter: sender, address, signature, then the P.S. (eye-scan tests)
1. **Finding.** About 90 % of readers look at the letter in the same order, so the P.S. is its real opening.
2. Siegfried Vögele, *Handbuch Direct Mail / Dialogmethode*, 1970s-80s. Summary only: Critical Fundraising, 24 Sep 2014, https://criticalfundraising.com/2014/09/24/opinion-the-forgotten-man-of-direct-marketing-fundraising/. Springer chapters "Blickverlauf als Gestaltungsgrundlage im Direct Marketing" (https://doi.org/10.1007/978-3-322-83509-3_11) located, not opened.
3. **Proof: C/E, summary only.** Film/video/eye-scan observation; N not given in the summary. Measured: viewing sequence.
4. Holds: printed direct mail, Germany, 1970s. Not tested on email or screens.
5. **Hook.** Email: put the benefit in the P.S.-equivalent (preview text) and from-name; letter: P.S. restates the offer. Channel: email / caption.
6. Quote: "it's the order in which 90 per cent of us will read any letter." Read: summary only.

## 24. A quiz/"IQ test" mailing outpulled the control by 138 %; a hidden-offer card by ~50 %
1. **Finding.** Asking the reader to score something (their copier's "IQ") beat a plain "Can You Afford..." control.
2. Caples 5th ed. (1997), figure caption ch. 18 (Pitney Bowes, 1995 CADM Tempo Award; tam.txt:15725-15759) and hidden-offer cigar card test (tam.txt:16450).
3. **Proof: E (award-entry claims).** Pitney Bowes: +138 % response and a higher sales conversion; N not given. Cigar mailing: ~+50 % replies.
4. Holds: B2B mail, 1995. Winner-reported results.
5. **Hook.** A tiny test the viewer can do in 5 s: "Score your kitchen: 3 questions". Channel: on-screen / caption / email.
6. Quote: "IQ outpulled the less involving 'Can You Afford...' control by 138 percent in response while converting to sales at a higher rate!" Read: section.

## 25. Schwartz and Hopkins: when the claim is not believed, open with what the reader already feels
1. **Finding.** A TV-repair-manual headline promising "Save up to $100 a Year" failed to make a profit; a headline echoing distrust ("Why haven't TV owners been told these facts?") then a qualifying question worked. Hopkins: sell the reward (beauty), not prevention; a free sample multiplied readers while "send ten cents" drew apathy.
2. Eugene Schwartz, *Breakthrough Advertising* (year not checked), Third Technique "Gradualization" (bt.txt:6955-7075; archive.org upload). Hopkins, *My Life in Advertising* (1927), "Pepsodent", pp. 161-163 (mlia.txt:4650-4725; https://www.mcgrinsey.com/media/claude-hopkins/claude-hopkins_my-life-in-advertising.pdf, read section).
3. **Proof: E/F.** Schwartz: "failed to make a profit" and the winner described, no counts. Hopkins: "We keyed every ad by the coupon. We tried out hundreds of ads", no counts.
4. Holds: 1950s mail order (Schwartz), 1910s US dentifrice (Hopkins). Schwartz's awareness/sophistication ladder is framework, no tests in the book.
5. **Hook.** Open on the reader's existing complaint, then qualify. Dentist: "Why does no one tell you why fillings fall out?".
6. Quote (Hopkins): "Prevention offers slight appeal to humanity in general." Read: sections.

## (a) Contradictions between sources
- Headline length: Rudolph (shorter read more) vs NYU/Ogilvy/Caples (long, news-bearing headlines sell more). Different outcomes (reading vs selling).
- Curiosity: Caples says curiosity is third and fails 12 of 13; Heath/Loewenstein and Whitman-era rhetoric favour a "gap"; Whitman himself cites a meta-analysis finding questions have "no appreciable influence".
- "Hook first, brand later" vs Teixeira 2014 (entertainment before the brand does not raise purchase intent) vs Romaniuk 2009 (brand early and often). These measure different outcomes (viewing vs intent vs recall).
- Langer 1978 (because) vs Folkes 1985 (no replication); Goldstein 2008 (norm) vs Bohner 2014 (no advantage).
- Ogilvy "FREE and NEW are the two most powerful words" vs Hopkins in hygiene ("free" sample worked; "send ten cents" drew apathy).
- TikTok/Kantar "3 s" claims (known) are consistent with Nelson-Field's 2-3 s threshold, but both come from platform/vendor datasets.
