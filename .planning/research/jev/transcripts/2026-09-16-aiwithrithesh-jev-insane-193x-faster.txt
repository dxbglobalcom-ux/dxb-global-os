So, a new AI model came out this week and it can't write text at all. It's called Jev. It comes from a startup called Type Safe AI founded by Diogo Almeida, whose work at OpenAI ended up as the research behind ChatGPT. Jev answers in under half a second. Input is 4.2 cents per million tokens and output is free. In this video, I'll walk you through what Jev is, why dropping text makes it so fast, the demos, the charts, what the first testers found, and the small print Type Safe wrote about their own numbers. Diogo's been chewing on one question for about 4 years. Chat models have been superhuman for a while now, so why isn't more work getting automated? He spent the last 2 years in stealth working on that. What came out is a new kind of model he calls a system one model. If you've read Kahneman's Thinking, Fast and Slow, you'll get the name. System 2 is slow, careful thinking. System 1 is the quick gut call. LLMs have gotten really good at the slow stuff. Jev goes after the quick stuff. The easiest way to picture Jev is as a function call. You pass in messy state, say an alert from your security tools or a support ticket, and you decide up front which answers are allowed. Maybe it's yes or no. Maybe it's a score or a pick from five categories. Jev hands back typed values with a probability attached to each one. There's no paragraph to parse, and by design, it can't reply with a type you didn't ask for. Type Safe compares Jev and LLMs in a big table, and four rows matter. Start with training. LLMs are tuned with RLHF or with rewards you can check, like passing tests. Jev uses a new method they call RLCD, reinforcement learning for calibrated decisions. So, the model gets rewarded when its probabilities are honest. Inputs are next. Chat models are built around a thread of messages, and Jev is built around program state. On outputs, you get strings from an LLM and typed values from Jev. The last row is sampling, and it's the big one. An LLM writes one token at a time. Jev puts out all of its answers in a single parallel pass. That's where the speed comes from. You can see it in their side-by-side demo. They send the same query to Jev and to GPT 5.6 Terra on default reasoning. Watch the Jev side, every field fills in at once while Terra is still working down the list. On this run, they disagreed on just one field, churn likelihood level, and Type-Safe says they aren't sure what the right answer is there either. To their credit, they also admit the query's been simplified. The input's short and the keys are easy to read, and that makes Jev look good. Okay, pricing. Input on Jev is 4.2 cents per million tokens, so $42 for a billion. Output costs nothing. For context, the post says frontier LLMs charge somewhere between 20 cents and $10 per million input tokens, and output usually runs about five times that. Speed's the other half. Jev takes 70 to 500 milliseconds end to end. Frontier models take anywhere from 3 seconds to over 5 minutes. Type-Safe puts that at 40 to 200 times faster on this kind of query. They also say pretty openly that they can't prove the price isn't subsidized. We'll find out over time. Their main chart comes from what they call workflow evals. Every model gets the same workflow written in code, and its answers are scored against the average of GPT-6, Astra, and Fable 5.1. This is the simplest of the four workflows. An alert comes in, the model decides if it's unauthorized, then whether to close it, queue it, or act, then reads the incident and picks a playbook. Now, look at accuracy against cost. Jev's way over on the left at about 68% and a run costs a tiny fraction of a cent. OpenAI's Terra gets roughly the same accuracy and pays around 70 times more. That's where the big numbers on their homepage come from. 193.6 times faster and 444.6 times cheaper. Type-Safe calls those the high end of what you'd see in practice. I'd slow down on this chart, though. Jev isn't the top dot. OpenAI's Soul and Anthropic's Opus 5 both score higher, somewhere in the low 70s. What Jev wins is accuracy per dollar. There's one more catch. Accuracy here means agreeing with Astra and Fable. So, the best Jev can do on this test is match them, and Type Safe's own capabilities team wrote these workflows. They point that out themselves, and they actually think the setup leans toward OpenAI and Anthropic. The next chart is about errors. For structured outputs, the LLMs range from about half a percent on OpenAI's smaller models all the way up to 45% for Haiku 4.5. For tool calls, GPT-6 Astra sits around 16%. Jev shows zero on both. Read before you get excited, though. That zero wasn't measured. Jev's output always matches the schema by design, so they just put zero on the chart. The LLM numbers also come from Openrouter, where harder queries might be going to bigger models. So, Jev can't hand you the wrong type. It can still hand you the wrong answer, and Type Safe never claims otherwise. All right, the demos. This one's Doom. Jev's playing in real time around 10 decisions a second, and that works out to about $7 an hour. Funny detail from the post, the engineer who built it was worried that was too expensive. Everyone else on the team thought it was cheap. A couple of caveats, Jev reads the game as a text data structure, so it isn't looking at the screen, and they admit a hand-coded Doom bot would play better. The point was a bot that follows instructions and reacts to the game fast enough to keep up. Then there's Wiki Racing. You start on one Wikipedia page and try to reach a target page just by clicking links. On a lot of pages, you're choosing from hundreds or even thousands of links. So, if a model makes one up, it's out. Jev usually got there in fewer steps. The speed gap is smaller in this demo, and Type Safe explains why. They ran the LLMs without reasoning with Astra on its lowest setting so the video wouldn't drag. One limit worth knowing, Jev can't pick from up to 255 options at once. When a page has more links than that, it scores them first and picks after, and that's the little pause you'll notice now and then. Early testers are posting too. One developer got access on launch day and ran around 5,000 requests. He used it for classification, model routing, intent detection and steering, and the whole lot cost him about $2. His latency matched Type safe's numbers around 150 milliseconds most of the time and about 350 on the slow calls. He says an LLM classifier used to take him about 4 seconds, so he had to write rough rules just to decide when to call it. With Jev, he can run it on every turn before the LLM replies and again after. He also noticed a pattern that feels backwards. With a normal LLM classifier, the more questions you pile in, the worse the answers get since they all share one reasoning scratchpad. Jev pushes you the other way. You split your query into lots of small independent questions and adding more didn't seem to hurt the rest. His plan now is to put a few Jev calls around every LLM call in his product. They'll decide when tools get loaded and catch bad replies before a user ever sees them. Another tester ran it on 1,500 of his own emails just to see how it copes with a real inbox. His words were that it's actually insane at email classification and he's blown away. It's one inbox, so treat it as a first impression. Still, sorting email is exactly the kind of dull, high-volume call Jev was made for. So, who's this for? Type safe's pitch is basically smart if statements for normal software. Classify this, route that, score it, pull out a field. They also talk about running it over huge data sets, dropping it into real-time apps where 100 milliseconds matters, and using it to check other models for guardrails and jailbreaks. It has real limits, too. It doesn't generate text, so it won't chat or write code for you. Access is early and goes through a waitlist. The service runs from the US West Coast and apart from a few early testers, every eval so far is Type safe's own. Last thing, the name. Jev is named after William Stanley Jevons. Back in the 1860s, he noticed that as steam engines got more efficient, Britain ended up burning more coal. Type safe thinks intelligence will go the same way. If a decision costs a fraction of a cent and comes back in a tenth of a second, you'll start putting AI in places you'd never put a chat model. My take, Jeff's real rival is the if statement in your code. And for that job, it looks very strong. The accuracy numbers still need independent testing. But giving up text to get fast typed calibrated decisions is the most interesting design bet I've seen in a model launched this year. As I always say, all this info points at a pattern, but check it on your own workload and see if it's useful for you.
