**3. The ten strongest openings**
1. "The Queen of Christmas Presents." (Bissell, letter to dealers): some 5,000 letters, 1,000 orders.
2. "Twelve woods to the dozen… never be offered again" (Bissell): 250,000 sweepers in three weeks. The letter wording is "in effect".
3. Biggest cake in the world (Cotosuet): 105,000 people climbed four flights in one week, after six weeks of flat sales.
4. "I have come from Chicago to consult you about a card": one man sold more in one week than six salesmen in six weeks, at a higher price.
5. "We Will Buy" instead of "10-Cent Cake Free" (Palmolive, 1911): the $700 test was repaid by repeat sales before the bills came due. Like results followed in numerous cities.
6. "No-Rim-Cut Tires, 10% Oversize" (Goodyear): ad spend went from $40,000 to nearly $2,000,000 a year. Goodyear's 1913 ad claims 918,687 tires sold and demand 12 times that of three years before.
7. Sunday coupon for a ten-cent can of milk (Van Camp, New York): 1,460,000 coupons presented. The $175,000 cost came back with profit in under nine months.
8. "Your name in gilt lettering" on each book: unprofitable ads became some hundreds of thousands of books, "with scarcely another change".
9. "Let us help you for a week without cost or obligation" (sewing-machine motor): about nine in ten trials became sales.
10. "Foods shot from guns", with the Prof. Anderson headlines: biggest earners in cereals by his account. The historian's number is below.

Best rule numbers:
- Cost per reply: $14.20 for a large, alluring ad against 42 cents for a plain one (41 cents in the other book).
- Chickens in silhouette: 50% more space, "not one added sale".
- Sample fetched at the shop 18–22 cents, by mail 70 cents.
- "Free" at the top of a hygiene ad divided results by four.
- Headline change alone, same ad: 5–10 times, from a file of nearly 2,000 headlines on one product.
- Fifty plans in five years for one food cut selling cost by 75 percent.

**4. Surprises and contradictions**
- **Nearly 2,000 headlines tested, and he prints almost no winning headline.** The book proves that headlines matter, not which ones won. No split-test pair of printed wordings with figures exists in either book.
- **Puffed grains.** Minnesota History credits the "nationwide sensation" to the rename to Puffed Wheat plus ads on Prof. Anderson and the "Eighth Wonder of the World". It puts the gain at nearly half a million dollars of 1909 profit. "He Invented Food Shot from Guns" is one of several headlines, so the guns line was not alone. CBC's "300 per cent" has no source.
- **Pepsodent.**
  - The museum history supports the sales: $200,000 in the first year to $6 million in 1922, and No. 1 within 18 months.
  - It contradicts his "I never referred to disasters". The first 1917 ads were headed "That Film on Teeth: The Source of All Tooth Troubles" and laid out like medical papers.
  - Gies found the paste did not remove the film. The museum, quoting Gies, dates that from 1917; the 1919 paper I saw only as an abstract. I left the look-alike ads out and flagged the Pepsodent row.
- **His two books disagree.**
  - Homes baking their own beans: 94 or 96 percent.
  - Announcing coupon dates: three weeks ahead in one, "we do not announce" in the other.
  - "Free": multiplies readers in food, cuts results to a quarter in hygiene, and lost to "we buy it" in a food pair.
- **"Positive beats negative 4:1"** has no table. He exempts troubles people already have.
- **Palmolive.** Cosmetics and Skin confirms disappointing sales until "an extensive advertising campaign", and the 1917 company rename. CBC says best-selling soap by 1916. Neither isolates an opening.
- **Van Camp and Goodyear dealers.** I found no independent figure for 1,460,000 coupons or 30,000 dealers. 1,460,000 "homes" looks larger than New York's family count (mine, UNVERIFIED).

**Left out on the door, with reasons**
- Schlitz is out as a row; only its method is kept, as a rule. That rule's only number is the beer's. His "again and again" for other goods names no figures.
- Liquozone: Collier's 1905 exposed it as 99% water. Only its coupon cost stays, as a rule with the doubt written.
- Reo "My Farewell Car": Olds did not intend to retire.
- Incubator booklet: ghost-written in a farmer's name.
- Instalment-credit clothing and furnishings; Racine shoe club; Hopper cosmetics (no opening with a result).
- Hosiery: there is no hosiery campaign in either book; only the brand name Holeproof is mentioned.
- Dr. Shoop's cough-cure line is kept but marked weak: the refund covered six bottles, and most buyers took one.
